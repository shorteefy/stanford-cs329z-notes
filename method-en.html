Method

How these lessons were written

This page describes the rules the lessons follow, where those rules come from, and how each lesson was checked. At the end it also says where the lessons fall short.

Who the reader is

The lessons are written for a working software engineer who reads code and knows school maths. ML and statistics terms are new to this reader. The goal is to understand the course technically, not just at the surface, and in the end to read the Stanford slides without help.

The teaching rules

  1. From first principles. Each new term is explained where it first appears. A term from an earlier lesson opens its definition when you click it.
  2. The concept first, then the failure. Each step first says what the new idea is and what it does. Then it applies the idea to a real failure, such as BM25 ranking the old forum post above the queue log.
  3. No analogies, a small version of the real system. All lessons run on the same small corpus: a course's office hours page, queue log, syllabus, an old 2024 forum post and the Homework 1 announcement. So the numbers stay consistent from one lesson to the next.
  4. Numbers before formulas. Every formula is first worked by hand on small numbers. Then each symbol in the formula is tied to those numbers.
  5. Maths taught where it is used. Logarithms, matrix products and rates of change all come in from school level, with numbers, and with a check wherever possible.
  6. A ladder. Each lesson opens with a list of steps. Under each step it says what you will be able to do after it, so you can skip what you already know.
  7. Connected sentences. Each sentence starts from what came before, and new information comes at the end. No line exists only to land a point, and headings name their content.
  8. The right form for each kind of content. Explanations are paragraphs, steps are numbered lists, and comparisons are tables. The most important sentence of each part is in bold.
  9. Think first. In many places the answer is hidden, so that you work it out yourself first.

The Hindi pages follow Spoken Hindi rules: Hindi words in Devanagari, technical terms in English letters, sentences of at most 25 words, and active voice. The English pages use plain English, and both languages carry the same numbers and sections.

Easy and dense

The easy version holds only the core: one worked calculation for each idea, and the maths that calculation needs. The dense version contains the whole easy lesson word for word. It then marks “From here: the Stanford level”, followed by steps that tie every symbol on the slides to numbers, read the paper's real setup and experiments, and finally have you read the slide images yourself, with questions.

How the writing went

  • 8 Oct 2026: a first version, told as a story along the slides.
  • 13 Oct: the first version read as choppy and full of punchlines. The research above produced the rules, and lesson 12 was rewritten in all four versions as the model.
  • 13 to 15 Oct: the other 17 lessons followed this model and one written brief, and every lesson was checked the same way. The brief changed three times after feedback: clearer maths, no references to the PDF, and dense = easy + the Stanford level.

Where the lessons fall short

  • The checks covered numbers, links and language, but they do not catch every mistake. Where you find one, trust the slides and the paper.
  • Some points are the author's own reading. On the page they are marked “my estimate” or “my”.
  • The slides and papers belong to their authors. These lessons do not replace them. They prepare you to read them.
