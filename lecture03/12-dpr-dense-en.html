Lesson 12 · Dense version

DPR: a retriever trained on question-passage pairs

DPR is a retriever that turns the question and every passage into vectors. It then picks the passages whose vectors have the largest dot product with the question's vector. Two encoders produce these vectors, and both encoders are trained on pairs of a question and its correct passage. DPR stands for Dense Passage Retrieval, and it is the retriever inside the RAG model from lesson 5.

The need for it showed up in lesson 11. For the question which office hour is least crowded, BM25 gave an old 2024 forum post 2.749 and ranked it first. The answer is in the queue log, which came second with 2.473, because it does not contain the words least and crowded. DPR's encoders are trained so that this question's vector moves close to the queue log's vector.

1Two encoders and a dot product

DPR has two separate encoders: the question encoder EQ makes the question's vector, and the passage encoder EP makes a vector for every passage. An is a model that reads text and returns a vector of fixed length. Both DPR encoders start from , and DPR them on question-passage pairs.

The score is the of the two vectors:

s(q, p) = EQ(q)⊤ EP(p)

The here is only a way of writing the dot product, and Stanford's slide writes it this way.

Now look at a small example. A real vector has 768 numbers. Here each has only 4, so you can do the arithmetic by hand. my vectors

The queue log's score comes from 1.0 × 0.2 + 0.5 × 0.4 + 0.5 × 0.4 + 1.0 × 0.4 = 1.0. The encoders are not trained yet, so the wrong forum post is on top with 2.0. Training in step 2 changes this order.

DPR reads the question and the passage separately, and the benefit is speed. Its work splits into two parts:

  1. , before any question arrives: the passage encoder makes each passage's vector once and stores them all in an .
  2. Online, when a question arrives: the question encoder runs once. Then the dot products with the index vectors are computed, and the passages with the k largest scores come back.

If one model read the question and the passage together, it would have to run once for every passage on every question. The Wikipedia from lesson 5 had about 21 million passages, so the model would run 21 million times per question. In DPR the model runs only once per question. This two-encoder design is called a bi-encoder.

Think first

Double the question's vector, so it becomes [2.0, 1.0, 1.0, 2.0]. What are the three scores now, and does the order change?

Show the answer

The scores become 2.0, 4.0 and 3.0. In a dot product, every multiplication takes one number from the question's vector. That number doubled, so every product doubled, and so did their sum: for the queue log, 2.0 × 0.2 + 1.0 × 0.4 + 1.0 × 0.4 + 2.0 × 0.4 = 2.0. Every score doubled, but the order stayed the same, and the forum post is still on top. This matters in step 2.

2Contrastive loss: putting the right passage ahead of the wrong ones

Contrastive loss compares the score of the right passage with the scores of the wrong passages. The further ahead the right passage is, the smaller the loss. Both DPR encoders are trained to make this loss small.

For this comparison, every training question needs two kinds of passages:

Positive
The question's correct passage. The training data lists it with every question. For our question it is the queue log.
Negative
A wrong passage for the same question. In our example, the forum post and the Homework 1 announcement.

The comparison matters because a retriever passes on only the top passages, so the order is what counts. In the question at the end of step 1 you saw that a longer question vector raises every score but keeps the order. If training only asked for a higher positive score, the encoder could take exactly that easy route.

Now compute the loss on the scores from step 1: queue log 1.0, forum post 2.0 and Homework 1.5. The loss takes three steps:

  1. Take for each score: 2.72, 7.39 and 4.48. e is a fixed number, about 2.718, so e1.0 = 2.72 and e2.0 = 2.718 × 2.718 = 7.39. The three add up to 14.59.
  2. Find the positive's share: 2.72 ÷ 14.59 = 0.186.
  3. Take −ln of that share: −ln 0.186 = 1.68. This is the loss. ln tells you the power you must raise e to in order to get a number. Check: e−1.68 = 1 ÷ e1.68 = 1 ÷ 5.37 = 0.186.

Steps 1 and 2 together are , and step 3 is the from lesson 4. The formula holds the same three steps:

𝓛 = − ln es+es+ + ∑ es−

On top is the positive's escore (2.72), and below is the sum of escore for the positive and all negatives (14.59).

Think first

Add 1 to all three scores: 2.0, 3.0 and 2.5. What is the loss now?

Show the answer

Still 1.68. Adding 1 to a score makes its escore 2.72 times bigger, because ea+1 = ea × e. For example, e2.0 = e1.0 × 2.72 = 7.39. The top and the bottom are both multiplied by 2.72, and the division cancels it. So the queue log's share stays at 0.186.

So the encoders have only one way to lower the loss: give the queue log a higher score than the other two.

3Negatives: from the batch and from BM25

Contrastive loss needs negatives for every question, and the more negatives there are, the stricter the comparison. But every negative has to go through the passage encoder, and that is expensive. DPR takes negatives from two places where this cost is low.

The positives of the other questions in the batch

In-batch negatives means treating the positives of the other questions in a batch as negatives for this question. Training runs questions in small groups, and each group is called a batch. The positive of every question in the batch is already encoded in that same step, so these negatives need no extra encoding.

Below is a batch of 3 questions, partway through training. In each row the bold number is that question's positive, and the other numbers in the row are its negatives. my scores

Hard negatives from BM25

A hard negative is a wrong passage that looks similar to the question but does not contain the answer. For our question, the forum post is exactly such a passage. It has both words, least and crowded, but no data about how crowded anything is.

In-batch negatives are mostly easy, because they are passages for other questions. Hard negatives produce a large loss, and the larger the loss, the more training changes the encoders. Partway through training the queue log is at 3.0, and the two negatives compare like this: my scores

The first row works out like this: e3.0 = 20.09 and e0.5 = 1.65, which add up to 21.73. The queue log's share is 20.09 ÷ 21.73 = 0.924, and the loss is −ln 0.924 = 0.079.

To find such passages, DPR uses BM25. For each training question it looks at BM25's top passages and takes one that does not contain the answer. This works because BM25 makes exactly the mistake the encoders need to unlearn. On our question, too, BM25 put the forum post on top.

Watch out: Check the labels before you take hard negatives. Sometimes a top BM25 passage really does contain the answer, and only its label is missing. Then the encoder learns to push a correct passage down.

4How far ahead of BM25 DPR is

The DPR paper measures a retriever by top-k retrieval accuracy: the percentage of questions whose answer passage appears in the top k. The NQ dataset holds real questions people typed into Google. On it, BM25's top-20 accuracy is 59.1%, and DPR's is 78.4%.

Out of 1,000 questions, that is 591 against 784. So with DPR, the answer passage reaches the reader for 193 more questions.

DPR is ahead on 4 of the 5 datasets. On SQuAD it is behind, 63.2 against 68.8, because there people wrote the questions after reading the passage. Those questions therefore reuse the passage's own words, and matching exact words is what BM25 does. this reason is in the paper

5Where DPR is weak

DPR wins at semantic matching across a whole corpus, but it has three weaknesses:

  • If a code like error 4012 appears in only one or two passages, BM25 treats it as rare and ranks those passages high. DPR's vector sums up the whole passage, and a single code like that is easily lost in it. Lesson 15 starts from exactly this case.
  • A passage's vector is made without seeing the question, so the question and the passage meet only at the final dot product. The reranker in lesson 14 works on this gap.
  • Training needs labeled question-passage pairs, even if only 1,000 of them.

BM25's strengths and weaknesses are the opposite of these. That is why lesson 14 combines the lists from BM25 and DPR.

The gradient: the loss's rate for each score

Lesson 4 introduced the : which way to change each parameter so that the loss goes down. There are hundreds of millions of parameters, so first look at the same idea on three scores, where the arithmetic is small.

Finding a rate is simple: change the input a tiny amount, see how much the output changed, and divide that change by the change in the input. On the scores from before training, 1.0, 2.0 and 1.5, the loss is 1.68. Now raise one score at a time by 0.01:

Score raised by 0.01Change in lossChange ÷ 0.01
Queue log (positive)−0.0081−0.81
Forum post+0.0051+0.51
Homework+0.0031+0.31

Take an even smaller change than 0.01, and these rates settle at −0.814, +0.506 and +0.307. The number a rate settles at is the gradient. The minus sign means the loss falls when the queue log's score rises, and the plus signs mean the loss rises when a negative's score rises.

This rate is written ∂𝓛/∂s: how fast the loss 𝓛 changes when the score s changes a little. The ∂ means that only this one score changed and everything else stayed the same. It is called a partial derivative.

Why these rates match the softmax shares

These rates match the softmax shares from step 2: −0.814 = 0.186 − 1, and 0.506 and 0.307 are exactly the shares of the forum post and Homework. This is no coincidence, and the reason takes five short steps:

  1. Split the loss into two parts. Lesson 4 gives two rules: ln(a ÷ b) = ln a − ln b, and ln(ex) = x. So 𝓛 = −ln(es+ ÷ Z) = −s+ + ln Z, where Z is the sum of all escore, here 14.59. Check: −1.0 + ln 14.59 = −1.0 + 2.680 = 1.680.
  2. The rate of es is es itself. Check: e1.01 − e1.00 = 2.7456 − 2.7183 = 0.0273, and 0.0273 ÷ 0.01 = 2.73, about e1.0 = 2.72.
  3. The rate of ln Z is 1 ÷ Z. Check: ln 14.60 − ln 14.59 = 0.000685, and 0.000685 ÷ 0.01 = 0.0685, which equals 1 ÷ 14.59 = 0.0685.
  4. Linked rates multiply. When the queue log's score changes, only es+ inside Z changes, at the rate 2.72. When Z changes, ln Z changes by 1 ÷ 14.59 times as much. So the rate of ln Z is 2.72 × (1 ÷ 14.59) = 0.186, which is the queue log's share. This rule of multiplying linked rates is called the chain rule. It is the chain rule for rates, not the probability chain rule from lesson 4.
  5. Add the two parts. In 𝓛 = −s+ + ln Z, the rate for the queue log is −1 + 0.186 = −0.814. The forum post's score does not affect the −s+ part, so its rate is just 7.39 ÷ 14.59 = 0.506.

So the rate for each negative is its own softmax share, which means training pushes each negative's score down as hard as its share. That is why the hard negative taught more in step 3. Partway through training (3.0, 2.0, 0.5), the forum post's share is 0.254, and Homework's is only 0.057.

One step back: the rate on the question's vector

One step behind the scores is the question's vector hq, with 4 numbers q1 to q4. A passage's score is q1p1 + q2p2 + q3p3 + q4p4. Raise q1 by 0.01, and the score rises by p1 × 0.01. So the score's rate with respect to q1 is p1, the first number of that passage's vector.

Changing q1 changes all three scores, and each score changes the loss at its own rate. With the chain rule, the loss's rate with respect to q1 works out like this:

  1. Queue log: score rate −0.814, times its first number 0.2, gives −0.163.
  2. Forum post: 0.506 × 0.8 = 0.405.
  3. Homework: 0.307 × 0.5 = 0.154.
  4. Sum of the three: −0.163 + 0.405 + 0.154 = 0.396.

Doing this for all four numbers gives [0.396, 0.304, 0.061, 0.081] my arithmetic. It is written ∂𝓛/∂hq, one rate for each number of hq. The first two numbers are the largest because those are the two large numbers in the forum post's vector, 0.8 and 1.0. In formula form the same arithmetic reads:

∂𝓛 / ∂hq = (p+ − 1) hp+ + ∑j pj hpj

Here p+ and pj are the softmax shares, and hp+ and hpj are the passages' vectors. The four steps above are the first number of this formula.

Now move the question's vector a little in the opposite direction: subtract 0.5 times its rate from each number. The new vector is [0.802, 0.348, 0.469, 0.959], and the scores become 0.871, 1.657 and 1.301. The queue log's score also dropped a little, but the forum post's dropped more. So the gap between them shrank from 1.0 to 0.786, and the loss went from 1.68 to 1.554.

The last step: back to the encoder's parameters

The question's vector is not itself a parameter. The encoder makes it, and the encoder has hundreds of millions of parameters. from lesson 5 applies this same chain rule backwards through every layer of the encoder, and gets one rate for each parameter. The list of all these rates is written ∇θQ𝓛i, and the is read "nabla".

Training nudges every parameter a little against its rate, exactly as we nudged the question's vector above. ∇θQ𝓛i changes the question encoder, and ∇θP𝓛i changes the passage encoder. These are the two red arrows on the slide in step 9.

7The whole batch in one multiplication, and the paper's experiments on negatives

The batch table from step 3 is really a matrix. A matrix is a table of numbers with rows and columns. Stack the vectors of the batch's questions one under another, and you get the matrix Q, one question's vector per row. The vectors of their positives form the matrix P in the same way.

Writing P⊤ means turning P's rows into columns, which is called a transpose. The rule for multiplying Q by P⊤ is: the number in row i and column j of the result is the dot product of row i of Q with row j of P. Here it is with 2 questions and vectors of 2 numbers: my numbers

Question ↓ / positive →p1 = [0, 2]p2 = [1, 0]
q1 = [1, 2]1×0 + 2×2 = 41×1 + 2×0 = 1
q2 = [2, 0]2×0 + 0×2 = 02×1 + 0×0 = 2

This 2 × 2 table is S = QP⊤. On the diagonal, row 1 column 1 and row 2 column 2, sits each question's own positive: 4 and 2. The other cells are its in-batch negatives. The paper gets all of a batch's scores from this one multiplication: from the paper

S = Q P⊤

With a batch of B questions, S holds B × B scores. Each row gets its own contrastive loss, so one step yields B training examples. In the paper B = 128, so one multiplication gives 128 × 128 = 16,384 scores, and each question gets 127 in-batch negatives.

The paper also adds one BM25 negative per question, and all 128 BM25 negatives of a batch become negatives for every question in it. So each question gets 127 + 128 = 255 negatives. The n in the slide's formula is this 255 in DPR's real training. from the paper

The paper's experiments: which negatives help how much

The paper tried several ways of choosing negatives on the NQ dev set. The dev set is a set of questions the researchers use to check their decisions during training. In 1-of-N, each question has its own separate negatives, not shared with the other questions in the batch. In in-batch training, the negatives come from inside the batch. from the paper

ApproachNegatives per questionTop-20 (%)
1-of-N, random passages764.3
1-of-N, BM25 passages763.3
1-of-N, positives of other questionsfrom the whole training set763.1
In-batch, batch 8769.1
In-batch, batch 12812773.0
In-batch, batch 128, plus 1 BM25 negative per question127 + 12878.0
  • In 1-of-N, the kind of negative barely changes top-20 accuracy: all three are between 63 and 64.
  • When the same 7 negatives come from inside the batch, accuracy goes from 63.1 to 69.1. The paper's explanation is that one multiplication inside the batch forms more pairs, so there are more training examples.
  • Growing the batch from 8 to 128 raises the negatives from 7 to 127, and accuracy from 69.1 to 73.0.
  • Adding one BM25 negative per question raises accuracy from 73.0 to 78.0. A second BM25 negative does not help: at batch 32, one gives 77.3 and two give 76.4.

These are dev set numbers. On the test set, the final DPR's top-20 is 78.4, which appeared in step 4.

  1. What does “Precomputed offline: One vector per passage” mean in DPR's row?

    Show the answer

    Before any question arrives, the passage encoder makes one vector per passage and stores it in the index. When a question arrives, only the question encoder runs. (Step 1)

  2. The cross-encoder takes 10,700 ms per 1,000 passages, and DPR takes tens of ms. Why such a gap?

    Show the answer

    A cross-encoder reads the question and the passage together, so it has to run the model once for every passage, and nothing can be prepared in advance. In DPR the model runs once per question, and the rest is dot products. The note under the slide says these numbers were measured on different hardware, so look only at the rough difference. (Step 1)

  3. DPR's row says “Breaks on: Rare literals; needs labeled pairs”. Which step covered these two points?

    Show the answer

    Step 5. A rare code like error 4012 gets lost in DPR's vector, and training needs labeled question-passage pairs, even if only 1,000.

3 points from this lesson

  1. DPR is a bi-encoder: two BERT encoders, with the score as the dot product of their vectors. Passage vectors are built offline into an index, so the question encoder runs only once per question.
  2. Contrastive loss compares the positive's score with the negatives: softmax, then −ln. The loss's rate for each negative is its softmax share, so hard negatives teach the most.
  3. A whole batch's scores come from one matrix product S = QP⊤, and one BM25 negative lifts top-20 from 73.0 to 78.0. Without extra pretraining, DPR beats BM25 on 4 of 5 datasets, but BM25 can still win on questions that hinge on exact words.

New terms in this lesson

DPR
Dense Passage Retrieval: a bi-encoder retriever with two BERT encoders, trained with contrastive loss on question-passage pairs. Built by Karpukhin and colleagues in 2020, and the retriever inside lesson 5's RAG.
Bi-encoder
Two separate encoders for the question and the passage, with the score as their dot product. Passages are encoded in advance. For example, even with 21 million passages the model runs once per question.
Pretraining
A model's first, large training on lots of general text. For example, BERT is first trained on general text.
Fine-tuning
Training an already trained model further on data for your own task. For example, DPR trains BERT on question-passage pairs.
Positive and negative passage
The question's correct passage (pi+), and wrong passages (pi,j−). For example, the least-crowded question's positive is the queue log, and its negatives are the forum post and the Homework announcement.
Contrastive loss
Softmax over the positive and negative scores, then −ln of the positive's share. It looks only at differences between scores. For example, scores 1.0, 2.0, 1.5 give loss 1.68, and 5.0, 2.0, 0.5 give 0.059.
Batch
A group of questions that training processes together. For example, a batch of 128 questions in DPR.
In-batch negatives
Using the positives of the other questions in the batch as negatives. For example, in a batch of 128 each question gets 127 negatives.
Hard negative
A wrong passage that looks similar to the question. DPR takes these from BM25's top passages. For example, the forum post for our question.
∂ (partial derivative)
How fast an output changes when one input changes a little and everything else stays the same. For example, ∂𝓛/∂s+ = −0.814: raise the queue log's score by 0.01 and the loss falls by about 0.008.
Chain rule (for rates)
Rates that are linked one after another multiply. For example, the rate of ln Z with respect to the queue log's score is 2.72 × (1 ÷ 14.59) = 0.186. Lesson 4's probability chain rule is a different rule.
ℝd
A list of d real numbers, that is, a vector with d dimensions. For example, [1.0, 0.5, 0.5, 1.0] ∈ ℝ4, and DPR's vectors are in ℝ768.
∇ (nabla)
∇θ𝓛: the loss's rate for every parameter in θ, as one list. Training nudges each parameter a little against its rate. For example, on the three scores these rates were −0.814, +0.506 and +0.307.
FAISS
An open-source library for fast search over vectors. For example, DPR built an HNSW index over 21 million vectors in it, which took about 8.5 hours.
ORQA
A 2019 dense retriever that trained its retriever with extra pretraining. For example, on NQ the full system's Exact Match is 33.3 with ORQA and 41.5 with DPR.
Top-k retrieval accuracy
The percentage of questions with an answer passage in the top k. For example, DPR's 78.4% on NQ at top-20.
