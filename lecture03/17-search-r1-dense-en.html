Search-R1: learning when and what to search

Search-R1 trains an LLM with reinforcement learning. After training, the LLM decides for itself when to search, what to search for, and when to stop. This training needs only the right answer to each question, not the search steps.

Take the question from lesson 6 again: Who is older, Jed Hoyer or John William Henry II? In the lecture's trace the model ran 3 searches and gave the right answer. But the training data holds only the answer for this question: John William Henry II. It says nowhere what the first query should be, how many searches to run, or when to stop.

1Learning from the final answer alone: rollout, reward and policy

In reinforcement learning (RL), the model makes several attempts at a question, and each attempt ends with a number. Training then raises the probability of the tokens in the attempts whose number was good. In lesson 4, instead raised the of a correct output written in the data. Search decisions have no single correct output, because the Hoyer question can be answered with 2 searches or with 3.

The final answer, though, can always be checked, and RL rests on that. Three RL terms come up throughout:

Rollout
One complete attempt at a question, from the start to the final answer: thinking, searches, returned passages and the answer. It is also called a trajectory.
Reward
The number at the end of a rollout. In Search-R1 it is 1 if the answer matches the right answer exactly, and 0 otherwise. This is the from lesson 5. from the paper
Policy
Whatever makes the decision at each step. Here it is the LLM, which picks every next token: think, search or answer.

Different rollouts for one question appear only if the model does not take the same path every time. So the model picks each token . Say that after the first <think> the model gives <search> a probability of 0.6 and a direct <answer> 0.4. Then out of 100 rollouts, about 60 start with a search and about 40 answer straight away. my probabilities

Now look at 4 rollouts for the Hoyer question. these four rollouts are my example

It lowers the probability of the tokens in o2 and o3, which include guessing without a search. Step 3 shows exactly how this raising and lowering works. 3GRPO: comparing each reward with its question's group

GRPO compares each rollout's reward with the average reward of the rollouts for the same question. Training then changes probabilities according to that difference. The simplest approach shows why: give each rollout a push equal to its reward. A push here means how much training raises the probability of that rollout's tokens. my example

QuestionRewards of 4 rolloutsPush equal to the reward
Easy1, 1, 1, 11 for each of the four, 4 in total
Hard0, 0, 0, 11 for o4 only, 1 in total

On the easy question the model is already right every time, yet it got a total push of 4. On the hard question the single correct rollout is the real lesson, and it got only 1. The probability of the three wrong rollouts did not even go down, because a push of 0 changes nothing.

So GRPO subtracts the question's average from each reward. All the rollouts for one question form a group, and their count is G, here 4. On the hard question the average is (0 + 0 + 0 + 1) ÷ 4 = 0.25. So o4's difference is 1 − 0.25 = +0.75, and the other three get 0 − 0.25 = −0.25. On the easy question the average is 1, so all four differences are 0, and nothing changes.

The size of the differences varies from question to question, so GRPO divides them by the group's spread. The measure of spread is the standard deviation, and it takes 4 steps:

  1. Take each reward's difference from the average: +0.75, −0.25, −0.25, −0.25. These always add up to 0, so their plain average cannot measure the spread.
  2. Square each difference, that is, multiply it by itself: 0.5625, 0.0625, 0.0625, 0.0625. Squaring removes the minus sign, so differences above and below the average no longer cancel.
  3. Average the squares: (0.5625 + 0.0625 + 0.0625 + 0.0625) ÷ 4 = 0.75 ÷ 4 = 0.1875.
  4. Take the square root, which undoes the squaring from step 2: √0.1875 = 0.433. Check: 0.433 × 0.433 = 0.1875.

Now divide each difference by 0.433. The advantage of o4 is 0.75 ÷ 0.433 = +1.73, and that of the other three is −0.25 ÷ 0.433 = −0.58. The advantage says how much better or worse than expected a rollout did. A positive advantage makes training raise the probability of that rollout's tokens, and a negative one makes it lower them.

In symbols, the same calculation reads:

Ai = (ri − mean(r1, …, rG)) ÷ std(r1, …, rG)

Here ri is the reward of rollout i, for example 1 for o4. mean is the group's average (0.25), std is the standard deviation (0.433), and Ai is the advantage (+1.73). GRPO stands for group relative policy optimization. If all the rewards are equal, std is 0, and GRPO sets every advantage to 0, because there is nothing to learn from that question.

4PPO's value model, and the KL penalty

PPO also trains with an advantage, and its expectation comes from a separate model called the value model. The value model reads the text so far and estimates how much reward will come at the end. PPO is the common method from before GRPO, and the Search-R1 paper tries both.

Suppose that on the hard Hoyer question the value model estimates 0.25. o4's reward is 1, so its advantage is 1 − 0.25 = +0.75. GRPO got the same 0.25 from the average of four rollouts. my numbers The difference is in the cost. The value model is itself an LLM, and it has to be trained too. GRPO does not need this model, but it needs G rollouts per question, and several searches in every rollout.

Both methods have one more safeguard. Training pushes the model towards reward, and the model can drift far from its old habits. So training keeps a frozen copy of the model from before training, called the reference LLM. At every token, training checks how different the new model's probabilities are from the reference's, and it puts a KL penalty on that distance.

For example, at some point the reference model gave <search> a probability of 0.2, and the new model now gives it 0.9. That is 0.9 ÷ 0.2 = 4.5 times as much, so the penalty is large. The model will change this much only if the gain in reward outweighs the penalty. my numbers

Expected value: an average weighted by probability

An expected value multiplies each possible outcome by its probability and adds the results. Suppose that on the Hoyer question the policy searches with probability 0.6, and every such rollout has reward 1. The other 0.4 of the time it answers directly and gets 0. Then 𝔼[r] = 0.6 × 1 + 0.4 × 0 = 0.6. my numbers

Training does not know these probabilities in advance, so it makes rollouts and takes their average. The four rollouts from step 1 give an estimate of (1 + 0 + 0 + 1) ÷ 4 = 0.5. The more rollouts there are, the closer this average gets to 𝔼[r]. The train reward in the slide's graphs is an average of this kind over the training rollouts. this is my reading, as the paper does not define train reward

KL divergence: an average of log-ratios

The KL divergence measures how different the probabilities are that the new model gives its decisions, compared with the reference model. The measure is an average over the decisions the new model actually takes. Take the example from step 4, where one point has only two options. The reference gives <search> 0.2 and <answer> 0.8, and the new model gives 0.9 and 0.1. KL takes three steps:

  1. Take each option's ratio, the new probability ÷ the reference probability: 0.9 ÷ 0.2 = 4.5 for <search>, and 0.1 ÷ 0.8 = 0.125 for <answer>.
  2. Take the of each ratio. ln gives the power you raise e to, and ln(a ÷ b) = ln a − ln b. So ln 4.5 = ln 0.9 − ln 0.2 = −0.105 − (−1.609) = 1.504. In the same way, ln 0.125 = −2.079.
  3. Weight these by the new model's probabilities and add: 0.9 × 1.504 + 0.1 × (−2.079) = 1.354 − 0.208 = 1.146.
𝔻KL(π ‖ πref) = ∑y π(y) · ln π(y)πref(y)

Here π(y) are the new model's probabilities (0.9 and 0.1), and the ln part is the log-ratios from step 2 (1.504 and −2.079). The adds over both options, and the result is 1.146. This is the 𝔼 from above: the average log-ratio, weighted by the new model's own probabilities.

A ratio above 1 has a positive ln, and a ratio below 1 has a negative ln. The option with the negative ln gets a small weight, because the new model gives it a low probability. 7The probability ratio and clipping: how much to change in one update

The probability ratio says how many times larger a token's probability is now than under the model that made the rollout. Clipping keeps this ratio inside a range, so that the model does not change too much in one update. The ratio is needed because rollouts are expensive: in every rollout the model writes up to several hundred tokens, and searches run in between.

So training uses one batch of rollouts for more than one update. In the paper the batch has size 512, and each update uses a mini-batch of 256. from the paper That makes 2 updates from each batch of rollouts. my arithmetic After the first update the model has changed, but the rollouts were still made by the old model.

The model that made the rollouts is written πold, and the model being trained now is πθ. Ratio = πθ(token) ÷ πold(token). At the first update the two are the same model, so every ratio is exactly 1. Training aims to make ratio × A large on every token the model wrote.

Now take o4 from step 3, whose advantage is +1.73. In it, πold gave the <search> token before the second search a probability of 0.5. my number If after the first update this becomes 0.55, the ratio is 0.55 ÷ 0.5 = 1.1, and the term is 1.1 × 1.73 = 1.903. If the ratio kept growing, so would the term, and the model would run far ahead on a single batch.

So PPO cuts the ratio off, which is called clipping. clip(ratio, 1 − ε, 1 + ε) follows three rules:

  • If the ratio is below 1 − ε, use 1 − ε.
  • If the ratio is above 1 + ε, use 1 + ε.
  • If the ratio is in between, leave it as it is.

In the paper ε = 0.2, so the ratio stays between 0.8 and 1.2, and ε is a hyperparameter too. from the paper The term for each token is:

term = min( ratio × A, clip(ratio, 1 − ε, 1 + ε) × A )

min picks the smaller of the two numbers. If the probability of o4's token becomes 0.75, the arithmetic takes four steps:

  1. Ratio: 0.75 ÷ 0.5 = 1.5.
  2. Without the clip: 1.5 × 1.73 = 2.595.
  3. With the clip, the ratio stops at 1.2: 1.2 × 1.73 = 2.076.
  4. min(2.595, 2.076) = 2.076. Even if the ratio rises above 1.2, this number does not change. So in this update training gains nothing by pushing the token higher, and the upper limit is 0.5 × 1.2 = 0.6.

With a negative advantage, the same limit applies on the way down. o2 wrote <answer> straight away without a search, and its advantage is −0.58. Suppose πold gave that <answer> token 0.5, and now it is 0.3. my numbers

The ratio is 0.6, and 0.6 × (−0.58) = −0.348. The clip stops the ratio at 0.8: 0.8 × (−0.58) = −0.464. min(−0.348, −0.464) = −0.464, which is the clipped term, so training gains nothing by pushing the token lower. The lower limit is 0.5 × 0.8 = 0.4.

8The mask on retrieved tokens: which tokens training learns from

Search-R1 computes its training terms only on tokens the model wrote itself. Tokens inside <information> are left out, and this is called retrieved token masking. For this, the paper puts a marker I(yt) on every token yt. If the model wrote the token, I(yt) = 1, and if it came from the search engine, 0. It works like the from lesson 13. from the paper

The terms of one rollout are then combined like this:

1∑t I(yt) ∑t: I(yt) = 1 termt

The ∑ in the denominator counts how many tokens the model wrote, and the other ∑ adds the terms of only those tokens. So this is an average over the model's own tokens, and termt is the min from step 7.

Count it on the slide's trace. The parts the model wrote, the four <think>, three <search> and one <answer>, hold 114 words in total. The three <information> blocks hold 105, 106 and 105, that is 316 words. my count, in words, not tokens With the mask, training learns only from the 114, while the other 316 are about three quarters of the text.

At the first update every ratio is 1, so in GRPO each token o4 wrote has a term of 1 × 1.73 = 1.73, and their average is also 1.73. The advantage is one number for the whole rollout, so every token the model wrote gets the same weight. The tokens of December 7, 1973, which have no evidence, also go up with this same 1.73.

Without the mask, two wrong things happen. In a rollout with reward 1, training also raises the probability of the tokens in the John W. Henry passage, so the model learns to write such text itself. In a rollout with reward 0, training lowers the probability of correct text that came from Wikipedia, even though that text was never the model's decision. The paper says the mask makes training more stable, and this experiment shows its effect: from the paper

9Where the advantage comes from: PPO's value model and GAE, and GRPO's group

PPO: the value model, GAE and an advantage for every token

In PPO the value model estimates at every token how much reward is still to come, and the advantage is the difference between the actual reward and this estimate. So different tokens of one rollout can get different advantages. In GRPO every token of o4 got the same +1.73. Below is the slide's trace, whose reward is 1, with the value model's estimate at four points: my estimates

Where in the traceEstimate V1 − V

The box on the slide that turns V and r into A is GAE, generalized advantage estimation. At every step GAE computes a small difference δ: that step's reward, plus the next step's estimate, minus this step's estimate. It then adds up all the δ from that step on. GAE has two more numbers, γ (gamma) and λ (lambda), and the paper sets both to 1. Then GAE is exactly this sum. from the paper

  1. δ1 = 0 + 0.6 − 0.3 = 0.3. The reward at the first step is 0, because the reward comes only at the end.
  2. δ2 = 0 + 0.5 − 0.6 = −0.1. The third search returned nothing useful, so the estimate fell.
  3. δ3 = 0 + 0.9 − 0.5 = 0.4.
  4. δ4 = 1 + 0 − 0.9 = 0.1. At the last step the reward is 1, and nothing comes after it, so the next estimate is 0.
  5. The advantage at the start = 0.3 − 0.1 + 0.4 + 0.1 = 0.7, which is 1 − 0.3. The estimates in between cancel, like the +0.6 and −0.6.

So with γ = λ = 1, every token's advantage is reward − V, such as 1 − 0.6 = 0.4 at the third query. With γ or λ below 1, distant δ get less weight, and this simple form no longer holds. The real reward also adds a small KL part at every token, which this example leaves out.

The value model is trained as well, which is why its box on the slide is yellow, and an arrow returns to it from GAE. In the first steps of training its estimates are not yet good. According to the paper, that is why PPO starts learning more slowly than GRPO. from the paper

GRPO: the paper's group of 5 rollouts

In GRPO the paper makes 5 rollouts per question, so G = 5. from the paper Suppose the rewards on the Hoyer question come out as 1, 0, 1, 0, 0. my numbers The advantage takes five steps, as in step 3:

  1. Mean: (1 + 0 + 1 + 0 + 0) ÷ 5 = 0.4.
  2. Differences from the mean: +0.6, −0.4, +0.6, −0.4, −0.4.
  3. Squares: 0.36, 0.16, 0.36, 0.16, 0.16, which add up to 1.2.
  4. The average of the squares is 1.2 ÷ 5 = 0.24, and √0.24 = 0.490. Check: 0.49 × 0.49 = 0.2401.
  5. Advantages: 0.6 ÷ 0.490 = +1.22 for the correct rollouts, and −0.4 ÷ 0.490 = −0.82 for the wrong ones.

Here the standard deviation divides by G, as in step 3. Some implementations divide by G − 1 instead: then the std is √(1.2 ÷ 4) = 0.548, and the advantages are +1.10 and −0.73. The signs and the order stay the same, and the paper's formula does not state this detail.

The paper's two full formulas

Now you can read the full GRPO formula. I write the ratio for short as ρ, where the paper writes out the whole fraction: from the paper

ρi,t = πθ(yi,t | x, yi,<t; R) ÷ πold(yi,t | x, yi,<t; R)
JGRPO(θ) = 𝔼 [ 1G ∑i=1G 1∑t I(yi,t) ∑t: I(yi,t)=1 min( ρi,t Âi,t, clip(ρi,t, 1 − ε, 1 + ε) Âi,t ) − β 𝔻KL[πθ ‖ πref] ]
  • (1 ÷ G) ∑i: the average over G = 5 rollouts, from this step.
  • (1 ÷ ∑t I) ∑t: I=1: the average over the model's own tokens, such as the trace's 114 words (step 8).
  • yi,<t and R: all the text before token t and the search engine, because each token's probability depends on them.
  • ρi,t, clip and ε = 0.2: step 7, such as a ratio of 1.1.
  • Âi,t: the advantage, which in GRPO is the same for every token of a rollout, such as +1.22.
  • β 𝔻KL: step 6, with β = 0.001.

The PPO formula is the same, only without the average over G and without the KL part: from the paper

JPPO(θ) = 𝔼 [ 1∑t I(yt) ∑t: I(yt)=1 min( ρt At, clip(ρt, 1 − ε, 1 + ε) At ) ]

PPO needs no group to compare against, so there is no average over G. At comes from GAE and changes from token to token. KL does not appear here, because PPO has already added it inside the reward. In both formulas training makes J larger. Training in lesson 4 made a loss smaller, and if you treat −J as the loss, the two are the same thing. That is why the paper calls the mask from step 8 loss masking.

The paper reports three findings. GRPO learns faster, because it does not have to wait for a value model to become good. PPO is more stable, while in GRPO the train reward drops suddenly after long training, which the paper calls reward collapse. The paper also varied the group size: 1, 3 and 5. With size 1 there is no group left to compare against, and according to the paper GRPO then becomes the older REINFORCE method. The average over 7 datasets is 0.410 with size 1, 0.363 with size 3 and 0.350 with size 5. According to the paper, a larger group learns faster but raises the risk of collapse, and size 1 did best on unseen datasets. from the paper

3 points from this lesson

  1. The search steps need no labels. The policy, that is the LLM, makes several rollouts for a question, and only the Exact Match reward on the final answer teaches it.
  2. GRPO builds the advantage from the group's mean and standard deviation. PPO builds it from a value model and GAE, which with γ = λ = 1 is reward − V. Both raise min(ratio × A, clip(ratio) × A) on every token the model wrote, and β × KL keeps the model near the reference.
  3. Retrieved tokens are masked, and without the mask the average over 7 datasets falls from 0.431 to 0.343. Search-R1 is far ahead of RAG on multi-hop questions, but the reward does not look at the steps in between.

New terms in this lesson

Reinforcement learning (RL)
Learning by making attempts and using each attempt's reward. For example, if 2 of 4 rollouts have reward 1, the probability of the tokens in those two goes up.
Policy
Whatever makes the decision at each step. Here the LLM, which picks the next token every time: think, <search> or <answer>.
Rollout (trajectory)
One complete attempt at a question, from the start to the final answer. For example, o1 on the Hoyer question: it searched both dates, then answered.
Reward
The number at the end of a rollout. For example, 1 if the answer matches exactly, 0 otherwise.
Search-R1
A method that teaches an LLM search decisions with RL, using think, search, information and answer tags. The reward is given only on the final answer.
Advantage
How much better or worse than expected a rollout did. For example, with rewards 0, 0, 0, 1, o4 gets +1.73 and the other three −0.58.
PPO and value model
A common RL method that trains a separate model (the value model) to estimate the coming reward. For example, an estimate of 0.25 and a reward of 1 give an advantage of +0.75.
GRPO
An advantage from the mean and standard deviation of a group's rewards. No value model is needed. For example, for 0, 0, 0, 1 the mean is 0.25 and the standard deviation 0.433.
Standard deviation
A measure of how spread out numbers are: the average of the squared differences from the mean, then the square root. For example, for 0, 0, 0, 1 the mean is 0.25, the squares are 0.5625, 0.0625, 0.0625, 0.0625, their average is 0.1875, and the square root is 0.433.
Reference LLM and KL penalty
A frozen copy of the model from before training, and a penalty that stops the new model from moving far from it. For example, if <search> goes from probability 0.2 to 0.9, the penalty is large.
Expected value, 𝔼[ · ]
Multiplying each outcome by its probability and adding, that is, the average over many rollouts. For example, 0.6 × 1 + 0.4 × 0 = 0.6.
KL divergence
The average of ln(new probability ÷ reference probability), weighted by the new model's probabilities. For example, 1.146 for 0.2 and 0.8 becoming 0.9 and 0.1, and 0.007 for 0.25 and 0.75.
Probability ratio
πθ(token) ÷ πold(token), how many times larger a token's probability is than under the model that made the rollout. For example, 0.55 ÷ 0.5 = 1.1.
Clipping and ε
Keeping the ratio between 1 − ε and 1 + ε, then taking the smaller term with min. For example, with ε = 0.2 and A = +1.73, the term for a ratio of 1.5 stops at 2.076.
Retrieved token masking
Computing training terms only on tokens the model wrote, leaving out the tokens inside <information>. For example, 114 of the 430 words in the slide's trace. Without the mask the average falls from 0.431 to 0.343.
GAE
Generalized advantage estimation: at every step δ = reward + next estimate − this estimate, then the sum of all later δ. With γ = λ = 1 the advantage is reward − V, such as 1 − 0.3 = 0.7.
Rejection sampling
Making several rollouts per question and fine-tuning only on those with the right answer. For example, 5 rollouts per question in the paper, with an average of 0.348 over 7 datasets.
