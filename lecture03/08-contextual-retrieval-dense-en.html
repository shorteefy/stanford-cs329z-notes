Giving a chunk back its lost context

The moment you cut a document into chunks, each chunk loses the text around it, and that text often said what the chunk was about. This lesson teaches two ways to give that context back. In contextual retrieval, an LLM writes a short context for each chunk. In late chunking, the encoder reads the whole document first.

In lesson 7, the queue log chunk office hour averaged 3. did not say which day it was about. This lesson looks at the same problem in the filings of 50 companies.

1Context loss: after cutting, a chunk no longer says what it is about

Context loss means that after cutting, a chunk no longer contains the words that say what it is about. A is the piece of a document that a retriever returns. A name often appears once, at the top of a document, so the chunks further down are left without it.

Take the example from the slides. The corpus holds the Q2 2023 quarterly filings of 50 companies, one per company. 50 companies is my example Almost every filing contains this line: The company’s revenue grew by X% over the previous quarter. The query is What was the revenue growth for ACME Corp in Q2 2023? ACME’s filing names the company and the quarter at the very top, so the chunk with the answer contains neither ACME nor Q2 2023.

Now count with lesson 2’s : how many of the query’s words also appear in the chunk. In lesson 2 the queue log scored 2, because it contained office and hour. After removing such as what, was, the, for and in, the query keeps 6 words: revenue, growth, ACME, Corp, Q2 and 2023. The ACME chunk contains only revenue, because it says grew, not growth. So it scores 1, and so do the other 49 chunks. my count

Think first

All 50 chunks score 1, and chunks with equal scores come out in random order. The retriever returns the top 20. How often does the ACME chunk make the top 20?

Show the answer

With a tie, the ACME chunk is equally likely to land in any of the 50 places. 20 of those places are in the top 20, so the chance is 20 ÷ 50 = 0.4, or 40%. The other 60% of the time the right chunk stays out of the top 20.

So the answer is in the corpus, yet the retriever misses it 60% of the time. An embedding does not save it either, because the embedding model reads only the chunk’s text. That text never mentions ACME, so the vector cannot carry anything about ACME.

Lesson 7’s keeps metadata with each chunk, but nobody will write metadata by hand for 60,000 chunks.

2Contextual retrieval: an LLM writes a context in front of each chunk

Contextual retrieval puts a short context in front of each chunk, written by an LLM that has read the whole document. Anthropic published the method in 2024. It runs four steps at , before any query arrives:

  1. For each chunk, give the LLM the whole document and that chunk.
  2. The LLM writes a short context for the chunk, 50 to 100 tokens long.
  3. Put that context in front of the chunk. The new text is called a contextualized chunk.
  4. Embed the contextualized chunk, and also add it to a word-based index, which comes in lesson 11.

For the ACME chunk, the LLM writes this context. The coloured part is the LLM’s, and the rest is the original chunk:

From ACME Corp’s Q2 2023 SEC filing; Q1 2023 revenue was $314M. The company’s revenue grew by 3% over the previous quarter.

Think first

The query’s 6 words are revenue, growth, ACME, Corp, Q2 and 2023. What is the contextualized chunk’s word overlap score now? What will the other companies’ contextualized chunks score?

Show the answer

The ACME chunk scores 5: revenue, ACME, Corp, Q2 and 2023. Growth is still missing. The other companies’ contexts carry their own names, so they score at most 3: revenue, Q2 and 2023. my count

With the context added, the ACME chunk breaks out of the 49-way tie and goes straight to the top. The embedding model now also gets a text that contains ACME and Q2 2023.

Anthropic’s results: failed retrievals@20

Anthropic measured the method on real data. Assume each query has one right chunk. Then failed retrievals@20 is the percentage of queries whose right chunk did not make the top 20. The smaller this number, the better.

MethodFailed retrievals@20Drop from 5.7%
Embedding, plain chunks5.7%baseline
Embedding + word-based index, plain chunks5.0%12%
Embedding, contextualized chunks3.7%35%
Embedding + word-based index, contextualized chunks2.9%49%

The drop in the third row is easiest to see on 1,000 queries:

  1. With plain chunks, 5.7% × 1,000 = 57 queries fail.
  2. With contextualized chunks, 3.7% × 1,000 = 37 queries fail.
  3. 57 − 37 = 20 queries are saved, and 20 ÷ 57 = 0.35. So failures fell by 35%.

The failure rate fell by only 2 points, from 5.7 to 3.7, yet failures fell by 35%. The drop in points is 5.7 − 3.7, and the percentage drop divides that by the starting value, 5.7. Add the word-based index as well, and failures fall to 2.9%, which is (5.7 − 2.9) ÷ 5.7 = 0.49, about half. How to merge the two indexes’ lists comes in lesson 14.

The cost comes at indexing time: one LLM call per chunk, and the whole document in every call. With 60,000 chunks, that is 60,000 calls. There is no extra cost at query time, because the context is already in the index. There is also a risk: if the LLM writes a wrong context, that mistake stays in the index.

3Late chunking: encode the whole document first, then average each chunk

LLM calls are expensive, so the next question is whether the encoder alone can supply the context. Lesson 7’s builds each token’s vector from the words around it. There, late had the vector [0.1, 0.9] in one sentence and [0.9, 0.1] in the other. But the encoder only sees the text it is given.

The usual method gives the encoder only the chunk: cut first, then encode each chunk on its own. This is called naive chunking. The chunk’s tokens know nothing about the rest of the document.

Late chunking reverses the order: the encoder reads the whole document first, and the cutting happens afterwards.

  1. Feed the whole document into a long-context encoder, an encoder that can read a very long text in one go, such as 8,192 tokens, about 10 pages. from the paper
  2. The encoder returns a vector for every token, and every vector is built with the whole document in view.
  3. Average the token vectors inside each chunk. That average is the chunk’s vector.

This average is lesson 7’s , for example [0.9, 0.1], [0.7, 0.3] and [0.8, 0.2] give [0.8, 0.2]. The cutting happens after encoding, that is, late, hence the name.

Take a small document: CS 329Z project milestone policy. It is due Week 6. The second chunk is It is due Week 6. In each vector, the first number says how much the text is about the project milestone, and the second how much it is about homework. my numbers

TokenNaive: chunk aloneLate: whole document
It[0.5, 0.5][0.9, 0.1]
is[0.5, 0.5][0.8, 0.2]
due[0.6, 0.4][0.9, 0.1]
Week[0.4, 0.6][0.9, 0.1]
6[0.5, 0.5][1.0, 0.0]
Mean pooling[0.5, 0.5][0.9, 0.1]

The last row works out like this:

  1. Naive, first number: (0.5 + 0.5 + 0.6 + 0.4 + 0.5) ÷ 5 = 2.5 ÷ 5 = 0.5. The second number is also 0.5.
  2. Late, first number: (0.9 + 0.8 + 0.9 + 0.9 + 1.0) ÷ 5 = 4.5 ÷ 5 = 0.9. The second: (0.1 + 0.2 + 0.1 + 0.1 + 0.0) ÷ 5 = 0.5 ÷ 5 = 0.1.

In the naive version, It cannot tell whether the text is about the milestone or about homework, so the vector sits in the middle. In the late version, the encoder also read the first sentence, so every token leans towards the milestone.

Take the query When is the project milestone due? with the vector [0.95, 0.05]. Lesson 3’s divides the dot product by the product of the two lengths, as in 0.74 ÷ (0.82 × 0.91) = 0.99. The cosine with the naive chunk takes three steps:

  1. Dot product: 0.95 × 0.5 + 0.05 × 0.5 = 0.475 + 0.025 = 0.5.
  2. Lengths: the query’s is √(0.95² + 0.05²) = √0.905 = 0.951, and the chunk’s is √(0.5² + 0.5²) = √0.5 = 0.707.
  3. Divide: 0.5 ÷ (0.951 × 0.707) = 0.5 ÷ 0.672 = 0.74.

For the late chunk [0.9, 0.1], the same arithmetic gives 0.86 ÷ (0.951 × 0.906) = 1.00. The late chunk points almost exactly the way the query does, while the naive chunk stays at 0.74.

Naive: (0.025 + 0.475) ÷ (0.951 × 0.707) = 0.74. Late: (0.045 + 0.095) ÷ (0.951 × 0.906) = 0.16.

The naive chunk gives 0.74 for both queries, so the milestone chunk ranks just as high for the homework question. my query

In the paper, with chunks of 256 tokens, late chunking raised the average score from 52.2 to 54.0. This is a ranking score from 0 to 100, which lesson 13 opens up. The gain is largest for the smallest chunks, because a small chunk loses the most context.

4Comparing the two methods: context in the text or in the vector

The two methods keep the context in different places: contextual retrieval writes it into the chunk’s text, and late chunking keeps it only in the vector. The other differences follow from this:

Contextual retrievalLate chunking
Who supplies the contextAn LLM, by writing textThe encoder, inside the vectors
Indexing costOne LLM call per chunkOne encoder run per document
Does the chunk get longer?Yes, by 50–100 tokensNo
Helps a word-based indexYes, the new words are in the textNo

The ACME query shows the difference directly. After late chunking, the chunk’s text still does not contain ACME, so its word overlap score stays at 1. So only contextual retrieval helps a word-based index, while both help embeddings. Late chunking also has a limit: the whole document must fit within the encoder’s length limit, or the context gets cut again.

Think first

The corpus is very large, the budget for LLM calls is small, chunks are 64 tokens, and the retriever uses embeddings only. Which method do you pick? What if the retriever is a word-based index only?

Show the answer

Late chunking. It needs no LLM call, and its gain is largest on small chunks. On a word-based index alone, late chunking does nothing, because its context never reaches the text. Then pick contextual retrieval.

5Anthropic’s full pipeline: the diagram, the prompt, the cost and the metric

Step 2 gave the four steps of contextual retrieval. Stanford’s slide shows the same steps in a diagram from Anthropic, and this step reads every box in it. The numbers in this step come from Anthropic’s blog post Introducing Contextual Retrieval, and the slide’s diagram comes from the same post.

The diagram runs from left to right like this:

  1. PREPROCESSING (new): this whole part runs at indexing time. New means a standard RAG pipeline does not have this step.
  2. Corpus to Chunk 1 … Chunk X: one document from the corpus is cut into chunks. X is simply the number of chunks.
  3. Run prompt for every chunk: one LLM call per chunk. The long blue line from below carries the whole document into this box, because the LLM needs the whole document along with the chunk.
  4. Context 1 + Chunk 1: the LLM’s 50–100 token answer goes in front of the chunk. The slide calls this prepended.
  5. Embedding model to Vector database: each contextualized chunk is embedded, and the embedding goes into a vector database.
  6. TF-IDF to TF-IDF index: the same contextualized chunks also go into a word-based index.
  7. Query: a query goes to both indexes. No LLM call happens at this point.

The box says TF-IDF, while the chart and the blog say BM25. multiplies each word’s count by a weight for how rare it is. For example, N = 6, n = 2 and f = 2 give 2 × ln 3 = 2.20. is an improved form of the same idea that caps the benefit of repeated words and lowers the weight of long passages. The blog’s index is a BM25 index, and the blog calls it Contextual BM25. from the blog

The callout at the top says that embeddings catch meaning, TF-IDF/BM25 catches exact words, and the system merges the results of both. Merging the lists of two retrievers is called . For example, BM25’s list E1, E3, E2 and the dense list E2, E1, E3 become one list. The blog merges the two lists with rank fusion, which comes in lesson 14. from the blog

The prompt, line by line

The prompt has four parts. {{WHOLE_DOCUMENT}} is a placeholder, an empty slot that the program fills with the whole document on every call. Then a line says that what follows is the chunk to be placed within the document. The program fills {{CHUNK_CONTENT}} with that chunk. The last part asks the LLM for a short context for search, and says the answer must contain nothing else.

That last condition matters, because the program puts the LLM’s whole answer, unchanged, in front of the chunk. If the LLM wrote a line like Sure, here is the context:, that line would go into the index with every chunk. In the blog’s prompt, the document sits inside <document> tags and the chunk inside <chunk> tags, which the slide does not show. Anthropic ran this prompt on Claude 3 Haiku. from the blog

The cost of the LLM calls, and prompt caching

Step 2 gave the cost in one line: one call per chunk, and the whole document in every call. Here is the count under the blog’s assumptions: chunks of 800 tokens, documents of 8,000 tokens, 50 tokens of instructions, and 100 tokens of context per chunk. from the blog

  1. One document has 8,000 ÷ 800 = 10 chunks, so 10 LLM calls.
  2. In each call the LLM reads 8,000 + 800 + 50 = 8,850 tokens.
  3. All ten calls together read 10 × 8,850 = 88,500 tokens, which is 88,500 ÷ 8,000 = about 11 times the document’s length.
  4. The LLM writes 100 tokens per call, so 10 × 100 = 1,000 tokens. my arithmetic

The biggest part of step 3 is the same document, repeated in all ten calls. Prompt caching makes exactly this repetition cheap. The LLM service keeps one part of the prompt after the first call, here the whole document. Later calls do not pay the full price to read it again. According to the blog, the whole cost then comes to $1.02 per million document tokens. from the blog

The index grows too. If a chunk of 800 tokens gets 100 tokens of context, then 100 ÷ 800 = 0.125, so every chunk becomes 12.5% longer. my arithmetic

The blog makes two more points. If the whole knowledge base is under 200,000 tokens, about 500 pages, put it straight into the prompt, and you need no RAG. The second point ties back to lesson 7: adding one generic summary of the document to each chunk gave very little gain. from the blog

6Late chunking from the inside: conditional embeddings, attention and the pooling formula

Step 3 showed the result of late chunking: the vector of It became [0.9, 0.1] instead of [0.5, 0.5]. This step shows how the encoder does that, and how the paper writes it as a formula.

i.i.d. and conditional embeddings

On the slide’s diagram, the naive half is labelled i.i.d. embeddings and the late half conditional embeddings. i.i.d. is a statistics term, short for independent and identically distributed. Here it means that every chunk’s vector comes from the same model, completely separately from the other chunks, that is, independently.

Conditional comes from lesson 4’s , where p(A | B) meant the probability of A once B is known, for example p(full | Tuesday) = 0.80. In late chunking, each chunk’s vector is conditional on the whole document: it is built with the document known. The dashed arrows between the chunk embeddings on the diagram show exactly this: every chunk’s vector is affected by the other chunks.

The naive box has an N in its corner. It means the box runs N times, once per chunk, and each time the model sees only one chunk. In late chunking the model runs once, and the small Token emb boxes are the vectors of the individual tokens.

How a token gets to see the whole document

Inside the encoder, each token’s vector is updated from the vectors of the other tokens, and does this. For each token, attention takes a weighted average of the other tokens’ vectors. In a weighted average you multiply each vector by its weight and add the results, and the weights add up to 1.

Suppose the starting vector of It is [0.5, 0.5] and that of milestone is [1.0, 0.0]. Attention gives milestone a weight of 0.8, and It itself 0.2. my numbers

  1. First number: 0.8 × 1.0 + 0.2 × 0.5 = 0.8 + 0.1 = 0.9.
  2. Second number: 0.8 × 0.0 + 0.2 × 0.5 = 0 + 0.1 = 0.1.

That is the late [0.9, 0.1] from the table in step 3. In naive chunking, milestone is not inside the chunk at all. Then It can only average over It, is, due, Week and 6, and they all sit in the middle. A real encoder does this over many layers, and the model works out the weights itself.

The paper shows the same effect with a real encoder. It cut the Wikipedia article on Berlin into sentences and computed the cosine between each sentence’s vector and the vector of the word Berlin. The model was jina-embeddings-v2-small. from the paper

SentenceNaiveLate
Berlin is the capital and largest city of Germany…0.84860.8495
Its more than 3.85 million inhabitants…0.70840.8249
The city is also one of the states of Germany…0.75350.8498

The first sentence names Berlin, so both methods give almost the same cosine. The second and third sentences only say Its and The city. There, late chunking lifts the cosine from 0.7084 to 0.8249, and from 0.7535 to 0.8498.

The paper’s pooling formula

The paper writes late chunking as an algorithm. A chunker cuts the text into chunks, measured in characters. A tokenizer turns the text into tokens and gives each token’s length in characters. The model returns a vector for every token, which the paper writes as ϑ: ϑ1 to ϑm, where m is the number of tokens. from the paper

The algorithm then walks through the tokens one by one, adding up their lengths in characters. When the total reaches the chunk’s length, the chunk ends there. This gives the position of each chunk’s first and last token, which the paper writes as cuestart and cueend. Each chunk’s vector is then:

ei = ∑j = cuestartcueend ϑj(cueend + 1) − cuestart

The is from lesson 3: keep adding from the position written below to the one written above, as in a1b1 + a2b2. Now run it on our document, treating every word as one token and leaving out the full stops. my simplification Then CS to policy are tokens 1 to 5, and It to 6 are tokens 6 to 10.

  1. For chunk 2, cuestart = 6 and cueend = 10.
  2. The sum on top, for the first number: ϑ6 to ϑ10 give 0.9 + 0.8 + 0.9 + 0.9 + 1.0 = 4.5.
  3. Below: (10 + 1) − 6 = 5, the number of tokens in the chunk. The +1 is there because counting from 6 to 10 includes both 6 and 10.
  4. The first number of e2: 4.5 ÷ 5 = 0.9, the late number from step 3.

Naive chunking takes the same average. The only difference is in ϑ: in naive chunking, ϑ6 to ϑ10 come from a run in which the model read only chunk 2. The model also adds special tokens such as [CLS] at the start of the text and [SEP] at the end, which belong to no word. The paper puts their vectors into the average of the first and last chunk. from the paper

Late chunking does not require training the model again. According to the paper, it works with any long-context embedding model that uses mean pooling, and with any chunking method. from the paper

Long late chunking: when a document is longer than the encoder’s limit

The slide’s title says Long Late Chunking. Recall the limit from step 4: the document must fit within the encoder’s limit. Many LongEmbed documents are longer than J2s’s limit of 8,192 tokens. For these the paper introduces long late chunking. Read the document in large windows of lmax tokens, and start each new window by repeating the last ω tokens of the previous one. from the paper

Suppose a document has 20,000 tokens, lmax = 8,192 and ω = 1,000. my choice of ω and document

  1. The first window reads tokens 1 to 8,192.
  2. The second window starts 1,000 tokens back, at token 7,193, reads 8,192 tokens and stops at 15,384.
  3. The third window starts at 14,385 and runs to the end of the document, token 20,000.

The first 1,000 tokens of the second window only provide context, and their vectors stay the ones from the first window. This way, the vector of token 8,193 is also built with the 1,000 tokens before it in view. The slide’s charts use this long late chunking. According to the paper, that is why the scores here are higher than in an earlier experiment, where the paper cut documents off at 8,192 tokens. from the paper

Where late chunking did not help

The paper finds two weak spots. On some reading comprehension datasets with large chunks, naive chunking did better. The paper’s guess is that the answer there is one specific sentence hidden in unrelated text. In the synthetic datasets Needle-8192 and Passkey-8192, a short useful fact sits inside unrelated text, and the surrounding context is of no use at all. from the paper

So late chunking helps only when the surrounding text really tells you what the chunk means. The company name in ACME’s filing is that kind of text. The paper also shows that chunking pays off even with a long-context model. On NarrativeQA with J2s, nDCG@10 was 32.73 without chunking at 8,192 tokens, and 47.63 with chunks of 512 tokens. from the paper This is lesson 7’s point: a lot of text blurs together in one vector.

8Both methods on the same ACME example

The paper compared late chunking with contextual retrieval in a small experiment, using the same ACME example. A made-up financial document has 5 chunks. The query is What is ACME Corp’s revenue growth for Q2 2023?, worded slightly differently from the slide’s query. The right chunk is It highlighted a 3% revenue growth over the previous quarter., which does not name the company. from the paper

The paper computed the cosine of each chunk with the query in three ways. For contextual retrieval, Claude 3 Haiku wrote a context in front of each chunk, and all three methods used the embedding model jina-embeddings-v2-small-en. from the paper

If the retriever returns the one chunk with the largest cosine, which chunk comes back for each method? Where does the right chunk stand under naive chunking?

Show the answer

Naive returns chunk 1, with 0.8505, because it names ACME Corp and Q2 2023 but has no growth figure. The right chunk is second, with 0.6343. Late puts the right chunk on top with 0.8516, and contextual with 0.8590.

In both methods the right chunk’s cosine is about 0.85. The paper’s conclusion is that the two methods give nearly the same scores, and late chunking needs no extra LLM. This is a small experiment on one document, so it does not settle which method wins on a large corpus.

In the contextual chunks, the LLM’s context can add words like ACME and Q2 2023, so a word-based index could also find the right chunk there. Late chunking leaves the chunk’s text unchanged, as step 4 showed.

The paper also offers a training method called span pooling. During training, the document’s vector is the average of only the tokens in the answer span. This teaches the model to pack useful nearby information into each token. The loss is like lesson 12’s , where scores 1.0, 2.0 and 1.5 gave a loss of 1.68. 3 points from this lesson

  1. Cutting makes a chunk lose its context. The ACME chunk ended up tied with 49 other chunks, and it makes the top 20 only 40% of the time.
  2. Contextual retrieval has an LLM write a context in front of each chunk. Failed retrievals@20 fell from 5.7% to 2.9%, and to 1.9% with reranking, a 67% drop. The cost comes at indexing time: without caching, the LLM reads about 11 times as many tokens as the document has.
  3. Late chunking builds conditional vectors from the whole document, then averages each chunk. In the paper the average score rose from 52.2 to 54.0, with the largest gain on small chunks. In the ACME test, both methods put the right chunk on top.

New terms in this lesson

Context loss
After cutting, a chunk can no longer say which document or topic it belongs to. For example, The company’s revenue grew by 3% does not name the company.
Contextual retrieval
Indexing each chunk with a 50–100 token context, written by an LLM, placed in front of it. For example, adding From ACME Corp’s Q2 2023 SEC filing.
Failed retrievals@20
When each query has one right chunk, the percentage of queries whose right chunk is not in the top 20. Smaller is better. For example, 29 failures in 1,000 queries is 2.9%.
Naive chunking
Cutting first, then encoding each chunk on its own. Then the vector of It is due Week 6 stays at [0.5, 0.5].
Late chunking
Encoding the whole document first, then mean pooling each chunk’s token vectors. Then the vector of It is due Week 6 becomes [0.9, 0.1].
Long-context encoder
An encoder that can read a very long text in one go. For example, 8,192 tokens, about 10 pages.
Prompt caching
The LLM service keeps a repeated part of the prompt after the first call, such as the whole document, so that part is cheap in later calls. According to the blog, contextual retrieval then costs $1.02 per million document tokens.
i.i.d. and conditional embeddings
i.i.d.: each chunk’s vector is built completely separately from the other chunks, as in naive chunking. Conditional: each chunk’s vector is built with the whole document in view, as in late chunking, where It gets [0.9, 0.1].
Long late chunking
Late chunking for a document longer than the encoder’s limit, read in large overlapping windows. For example, 20,000 tokens read in windows of 8,192 tokens, each new window starting 1,000 tokens back.
