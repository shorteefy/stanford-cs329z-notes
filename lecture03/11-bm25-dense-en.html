BM25: a score built from rare words, counts and length

BM25 is a retriever that gives a passage a weight for each word of the query. The sum of these weights is the passage's score. Each weight comes from three things: how rare the word is in the whole corpus, how many times it appears in the passage, and how long the passage is. Stanford's slide calls it the most widely used retriever, even today.

The need for it showed up in lesson 2. counts how many query words also appear in a passage. For the query which office hour is least crowded it gave two passages the same score of 2. One is the queue log, which holds the crowd data and the answer. The other is an old forum post from 2024, which holds only an opinion. This lesson adds BM25's three parts one at a time and checks the tie each time.

1IDF: more weight for rare terms

IDF gives every word a weight, and the fewer passages a word appears in, the bigger its weight. In search, each such word of the query is called a term. are very common words such as which and is, which are removed before counting. After removing them, our query has four terms left: office, hour, least and crowded.

Office appears in two passages, and least and crowded only in the forum post, yet word overlap counts all four the same. The number of passages a term appears in is called its document frequency, written n. Our corpus has N = 6 passages, and milestone appears only in the syllabus, so n = 1. Its IDF takes three steps:

  1. Count the passages without the term and add 0.5: 6 − 1 + 0.5 = 5.5.
  2. Count the passages with the term and add 0.5 as well: 1 + 0.5 = 1.5.
  3. Divide the first number by the second, 5.5 ÷ 1.5 = 3.667, and take its ln: ln 3.667 = 1.299.

The ratio in step 3 says how many passages lack the term for each passage that has it. The 0.5 is added so that the ratio still exists when a count is 0.

The in step 3 comes from lesson 4. ln x is the power you raise e = 2.718 to in order to get x. Check: e1.2993 = 3.667, so ln 3.667 = 1.299. One rule will be useful below: ln 1 = 0, because e0 = 1.

This weight is called IDF, short for inverse document frequency: the smaller n is, the bigger the IDF. The formula holds the same three steps:

IDF = ln N − n + 0.5n + 0.5

Thursday is in half the passages, so its ratio is 1, and since ln 1 = 0, its weight is 0.

Now take the terms of our query. Office has an IDF of 0.588. Hour appears only in the queue log, because the office hours page says hours, not hour, and the retriever treats these as different words. So hour, least and crowded all have n = 1 and an IDF of 1.299.

But office hour appears twice in the queue log, for Tuesday and for Thursday, and IDF counts it only once. The next step adds this count.

2Term frequency and saturation: a count that pays off only up to a ceiling

The term frequency f is how many times a term appears in one passage. In the queue log, office and hour both have f = 2. But the first appearance tells you the passage is about that term, and the tenth adds very little to that. So BM25 does not simply multiply by f.

It turns f into a new number, which this lesson calls the TF part. Leave passage length aside for now, it comes in step 3:

TF part = f × 2.2f + 1.2

For f = 2 the arithmetic takes three steps:

  1. Top: 2 × 2.2 = 4.4.
  2. Bottom: 2 + 1.2 = 3.2.
  3. Divide: 4.4 ÷ 3.2 = 1.375.

Check: at f = 1 the top is 2.2 and the bottom is 2.2, so the TF part is exactly 1. Now let f grow:

fTF part

Going from 1 to 2 appearances adds 0.375, while going from 5 to 10 adds only 0.190. As f grows, the 1.2 in the bottom becomes small next to it, so the TF part gets close to 2.2 but never reaches it. This is called saturation: each extra appearance adds less than the one before, and the TF part never goes above a ceiling.

The 1.2 in the formula is a knob called k1, and the ceiling 2.2 is really k1 + 1. A small k1 means a low ceiling that fills up quickly. k1 = 1.2 is my choice, and the slide's graph has this line too

Now back to the queue log. Office and hour both appear twice, so both have a TF part of 1.375, and the score is 0.588 × 1.375 + 1.299 × 1.375 = 2.595. Both of the forum post's terms appear once, so its score stays at 2.599. Adding the count brings the queue log almost level, with a gap of only 0.004.

Think first

What does the TF part become when k1 = 0? How does that relate to the scores in step 1?

Show the answer

The formula becomes f × 1 ÷ (f + 0), that is, f ÷ f = 1, as long as f is at least 1. That is the word overlap of lesson 2, only with IDF weights. The 2.599 and 1.887 from step 1 were exactly these k1 = 0 scores.

3Length normalization: less weight for a term in a long passage

Length normalization lowers the TF part of a term in a long passage, and raises it for a term in a short passage. Office appearing once in a 1,000-word document says less than office appearing once in a 20-word passage.

For this, BM25 compares each passage's length with the average length in the corpus. A passage's length in words is written |D|, and the average length is avgdl. An average is the sum of all the lengths divided by the number of passages:

avgdl = (27 + 16 + 19 + 14 + 8 + 13) ÷ 6 = 97 ÷ 6 = 16.17

Check: 16.17 × 6 = 97.02, which is about 97. Now BM25 multiplies the k1 in the bottom of the TF part by this factor:

factor = 1 − b + b × |D|avgdl

|D| ÷ avgdl says how many times the average a passage is. b is a second knob, between 0 and 1, that sets how much length matters. For the queue log, with b = 0.75, the factor takes three steps: b = 0.75 is my choice

  1. |D| ÷ avgdl = 19 ÷ 16.17 = 1.175.
  2. Multiply by b: 0.75 × 1.175 = 0.881.
  3. Add 1 − b: 0.25 + 0.881 = 1.131.

Check: at average length the factor is 0.25 + 0.75 = 1, so nothing changes. A longer passage has a factor above 1, so its TF part shrinks. At f = 1, for example, a passage twice the average length drops from 1.000 to 0.710.

This is called length normalization. With b = 0 the factor is always 1, so length has no effect, and with b = 1 length has its full effect. In our corpus the queue log has 19 words, more than average, so its factor is 1.131. The forum post has 14 words, so its factor is 0.25 + 0.75 × 14 ÷ 16.17 = 0.899.

Think first

Say b = 1 and the passage is twice the average length. What is the TF part at f = 1 and k1 = 1.2?

Show the answer

Factor = 1 − 1 + 1 × 2 = 2. TF part = 2.2 ÷ (1 + 1.2 × 2) = 2.2 ÷ 3.4 = 0.647. That is even less than the 0.710 at b = 0.75, because length now has its full effect.

4All three parts in one formula: BM25

The BM25 score multiplies each query term's IDF by its TF part, and adds this up over all the terms. A term that is not in the passage contributes 0. Do the whole calculation for office in the queue log: f = 2, and the factor from step 3, 1.1314 to 4 decimals.

  1. Bottom: 2 + 1.2 × 1.1314 = 2 + 1.3577 = 3.3577.
  2. Top: 2 × 2.2 = 4.4.
  3. TF part: 4.4 ÷ 3.3577 = 1.3104.
  4. Multiply by the IDF: 0.5878 × 1.3104 = 0.7703.

For least in the forum post the factor is 0.8995, so the TF part is 2.2 ÷ (1 + 1.2 × 0.8995) = 2.2 ÷ 2.0794 = 1.0580. For the query which office hour is least crowded, with k1 = 1.2 and b = 0.75, the full calculation is:

Stanford's slide writes the same calculation as one formula:

score(D, Q) = ∑i = 1 … n IDF(qi) · f(qi, D) · (k1 + 1)f(qi, D) + k1 · (1 − b + b · |D| ÷ avgdl)

The comes from lesson 3. It means: for every i from the number written below up to the n written above, work out what follows, and add it all up. Here i runs from 1 to 4: 0.7703 + 1.7026 + 0 + 0 = 2.473. The “·” in the formula is a multiplication sign, D is the passage being scored, and qi is the i-th query term. The other parts come from steps 1 to 3.

5The inverted index: why BM25 is fast, and where it fails

An inverted index is a list, for each term, of the passages that contain it. BM25 builds these lists , that is, once, before any question arrives. The lists for our query's terms look like this:

  • office → office hours page, queue log
  • hour → queue log
  • least → old forum post
  • crowded → old forum post

When a question arrives, BM25 opens only these 4 lists and never touches the other 3 passages. That is why it is fast: on Stanford's comparison slide it answers in 62 ms on a large dataset.

To BM25, every passage is a long vector with one slot for each word in the vocabulary. The forum post has 14 of our 69 words, so 55 of its slots are 0, and a vector like this is called sparse. The of lesson 3 are dense: 768 numbers, almost none of them 0.

According to the same row, BM25 wins on exact terms, such as IDs, names and error codes. A code like E-4012 is either in a passage or not, and because it is rare, its IDF is high.

BM25 breaks on paraphrase and synonyms, that is, the same thing said in other words. The answer to the widget's third query is on the office hours page, but that page says TAs, not ta, and help appears nowhere. So every score is 0.

The forum post mistake comes from the same place, because BM25 only counts words. Lesson 12 builds a retriever that learns from question-passage pairs, and lesson 14 combines both lists.

What the k1 + 1 on top does

The k1 + 1 in the numerator multiplies every term's TF part by the same number, 2.2. So every passage's whole score is multiplied by 2.2 as well, and the order of the passages does not change. If you drop k1 + 1, for example, the forum post comes to 2.749 ÷ 2.2 = 1.250 and the queue log to 2.473 ÷ 2.2 = 1.124. The forum post is still on top.

According to Robertson and Zaragoza, many versions keep k1 + 1 so that a term that appears once in an average-length passage gets exactly its IDF as its weight. from the paper With our numbers: at f = 1 and factor = 1 the TF part is 2.2 ÷ 2.2 = 1, so the weight is IDF × 1.

Shrinking the count by length

There is a second way to read the length factor. It needs one rule about ratios: dividing the top and the bottom by the same number does not change a ratio. For example, 6 ÷ 3 = 2, and (6 ÷ 2) ÷ (3 ÷ 2) = 3 ÷ 1.5 = 2.

Divide the top and the bottom of the TF part by the factor. The k1 × factor in the bottom becomes just k1, and f is replaced by f ÷ factor:

f · (k1 + 1)f + k1 · factor = (f ÷ factor) · (k1 + 1)(f ÷ factor) + k1

So BM25 first shrinks the count by length, and then applies the saturation from step 2. Robertson and Zaragoza write BM25 exactly this way. from the paper For office in the queue log it takes three steps:

  1. The shrunk count: 2 ÷ 1.1314 = 1.7677.
  2. The formula from step 2: top 1.7677 × 2.2 = 3.8889, bottom 1.7677 + 1.2 = 2.9677.
  3. Divide: 3.8889 ÷ 2.9677 = 1.3104, the same TF part as in step 4.

In the forum post, the single count of least becomes 1 ÷ 0.8995 = 1.1117, and 1.1117 × 2.2 ÷ (1.1117 + 1.2) = 2.4457 ÷ 2.3117 = 1.0580. Length turned the queue log's 2 counts into something like 1.77, and the forum post's 1 count into something like 1.11.

The edges of IDF: two more ln rules, negative IDF and TF-IDF

Step 1 gave one rule of ln: ln 1 = 0. Reading the slide's IDF graph needs two more:

  • The ln of a number below 1 is negative, and flipping a ratio only flips the sign of its ln. For example, ln 2 = 0.693, so ln(1 ÷ 2) = −0.693, because e−0.693 = 0.5.
  • The ln of a product is the sum of the lns: ln(a × b) = ln a + ln b. For example, ln 4 = ln(2 × 2) = 0.693 + 0.693 = 1.386.

The second rule means that when a ratio grows 10 times, its ln grows by only ln 10 = 2.303. So ln squeezes very large ratios into small weights. A term that appears in just 1 of 10,000 passages has a ratio of 9,999.5 ÷ 1.5 = 6,666, but an IDF of only 8.805.

The first rule produces negative IDF. A term that appears in more than half the passages has a ratio below 1. A term in 5 of the 6 passages, for example, has the ratio 1.5 ÷ 5.5, which is milestone's ratio 5.5 ÷ 1.5 flipped, so its IDF is −1.299. At the other end, for a term that appears in no passage, the ratio without the 0.5 would be 6 ÷ 0, which has no value.

The older TF-IDF, which came before BM25, uses the weight ln(N ÷ n). N ÷ n says the term appears in one passage out of how many. Office is in 2 of 6 passages, that is, 1 in every 3, so its weight is ln 3 = 1.099. This weight is never negative, and TF-IDF multiplies it directly by the count: for office in the queue log, 2 × 1.099 = 2.20.

Where the 0.5 comes from: the RSJ weight worked out in full

BM25's IDF comes from an older weight by Robertson and Spärck Jones, called the RSJ weight. from the paper The RSJ weight is built from four counts. N and n are the same as before. R is the number of documents that are really useful for the query, called relevant. r is how many of those relevant documents contain the term.

w = ln (r + 0.5) · (N − R − n + r + 0.5)(n − r + 0.5) · (R − r + 0.5)

First, suppose we know that the syllabus is relevant to a question about the milestone. Then R = 1 and r = 1, with N = 6 and n = 1:

  1. r + 0.5 = 1.5.
  2. N − R − n + r + 0.5 = 6 − 1 − 1 + 1 + 0.5 = 5.5.
  3. n − r + 0.5 = 1 − 1 + 0.5 = 0.5.
  4. R − r + 0.5 = 1 − 1 + 0.5 = 0.5.
  5. Divide: (1.5 × 5.5) ÷ (0.5 × 0.5) = 8.25 ÷ 0.25 = 33, and ln 33 = 3.497.

BM25 does not know which documents are relevant. Then R = 0 and r = 0:

  1. r + 0.5 = 0.5.
  2. N − R − n + r + 0.5 = 6 − 0 − 1 + 0 + 0.5 = 5.5.
  3. n − r + 0.5 = 1 + 0.5 = 1.5.
  4. R − r + 0.5 = 0.5.
  5. Divide: (0.5 × 5.5) ÷ (1.5 × 0.5) = 2.75 ÷ 0.75 = 3.667, and ln 3.667 = 1.299, exactly milestone's IDF.

In the second calculation the 0.5 on top and the 0.5 below cancel, and ln((N − n + 0.5) ÷ (n + 0.5)) is left. Without the 0.5s this calculation would be (0 × 5) ÷ (1 × 0) = 0 ÷ 0, which has no value. That is why the paper adds 0.5 to every count, so that the ratio and the log never break. from the paper

8Who picks k1 and b

k1 and b are hyperparameters: BM25 does not learn them from data, the person building the search picks them. A hyperparameter is a number in a formula or model that training does not change. BM25 has no training at all, so k1 and b are the only numbers in it that anyone chooses.

The usual method is to set aside some questions whose correct passages are known. Then try many values of k1 and b, see how high the correct passages come out, and keep the best pair. Such a set of questions is called a dev set, short for development set. Lesson 13 teaches how to measure whether the correct passages came out on top.

According to Robertson and Zaragoza, many experiments find that b from 0.5 to 0.8 and k1 from 1.2 to 2 work well. They also say the best values depend on the kind of documents and queries. from the paper

The paper also explains why b sits between 0 and 1. A document can be long for two reasons. One, the author says the same thing in more words, and then dividing the count fully by length is right, that is, b = 1. Two, the document really covers more topics, and then dividing by length is wrong, that is, b = 0. Real documents mix both reasons. from the paper

The DPR paper ran Lucene's BM25 on Wikipedia passages and picked k1 = 0.9 and b = 0.4 on its dev sets. Both values lie outside the range above. from the paper Even with these values the order on our query stays the same: forum post 2.666, queue log 2.420. my arithmetic

Show the answer

Raise b and lower k1. With k1 = 1.2 and b = 0.75 the guide's factor is 0.25 + 0.75 × 10 = 7.75, and its TF part is 20 × 2.2 ÷ (20 + 1.2 × 7.75) = 44 ÷ 29.3 = 1.502. An average-length article with the term once has a TF part of 1.000.

With b = 1 the guide's factor is 10, and its TF part is 44 ÷ (20 + 12) = 1.375. Setting k1 = 0.5 as well gives 20 × 1.5 ÷ (20 + 0.5 × 10) = 30 ÷ 25 = 1.200. The article stays at 1.000, so the gap shrinks from 0.502 to 0.200. Then measure on a dev set whether the right articles come out on top.

9BM25 at real scale

In a real inverted index, every entry in a term's list stores the passage together with its f. The lists for our query's terms look like this:

  • office → (office hours page, f = 1), (queue log, f = 2)
  • hour → (queue log, f = 2)
  • least → (old forum post, f = 1)
  • crowded → (old forum post, f = 1)

The length of a list is n. Office's list has 2 entries, for example, so n = 2, and the IDF follows from that. The index also stores each passage's |D| and the corpus's avgdl. When a question arrives, BM25 reads 5 entries across 4 lists and scores only 3 passages.

BM25 as a dot product of sparse vectors

According to the DPR paper, TF-IDF and BM25 treat the question and the passage as very long sparse vectors, with a weight for each word. from the paper To see this you need the from lesson 3. A dot product multiplies the numbers in the same position of two vectors and adds them up. For example, the dot product of [1, 0, 1] and [2, 5, 3] is 1 × 2 + 0 × 5 + 1 × 3 = 5.

For our corpus, make a vector with 69 numbers, one for each word in the vocabulary. In a passage's vector, each word's slot holds its weight, IDF × TF part, and the slot of a word not in the passage holds 0. In the query's vector, the slots of the 4 query terms hold 1, and the other 65 slots hold 0.

The dot product of these two vectors is exactly the BM25 score, because a product is non-zero only where both vectors have a number. For the forum post those are the two slots for least and crowded: 1 × 1.3747 + 1 × 1.3747 = 2.749. In the other 67 slots, at least one side is 0. An inverted index is really a way of storing these sparse vectors by term, so that the zero slots never have to be read.

What data the 62 ms was measured on

Below Stanford's comparison slide it says the latencies come from Khattab and Zaharia (2020), the ColBERT paper, on MS MARCO. In that paper, 62 ms is the number for Anserini's BM25, and Anserini is a search toolkit built on Lucene. from the paper

MS MARCO holds 8.8 million passages from web pages, gathered from Bing's results for 1 million real questions. In the paper each query returned the top 1,000 of these 8.8 million passages, and latency was measured on one CPU thread. According to the paper, BM25 is this fast because its steps are cheap and this kind of search has been optimized for decades. from the paper

In the DPR paper, Lucene's BM25 handles 23.7 questions per second on one CPU thread. Since 1 second = 1,000 ms, that is 1,000 ÷ 23.7 = 42 ms per question. Its inverted index over 21 million passages takes about 30 minutes to build, while DPR's vectors and index take 8.8 + 8.5 = 17.3 hours. from the paper The two papers use different hardware and corpora, so 62 ms and 42 ms cannot be compared directly, only that both are in the tens of milliseconds.

BM25's strength and weakness in the papers

The DPR paper says methods like BM25 are sensitive to highly selective keywords and phrases. from the paper The IDs, names and error codes in the slide's row are words of this kind. An example from the same paper shows the weakness. The question is: Who is the bad guy in lord of the rings? Its answer is in this passage: Sala Baker is best known for portraying the villain Sauron in the Lord of the Rings trilogy.

The question says bad guy, and the passage says villain. So, according to the paper, a term-based system would have difficulty finding this passage, while a dense retriever can bring the vectors for bad guy and villain close together. from the paper This is a real example of the slide's Breaks on: Paraphrase, synonyms.

Read the slide on types of retrievers

  1. The slide calls BM25 word-overlap. What three things does BM25 add to the word overlap of lesson 2?

    Show the answer

    More weight for rare terms (IDF), a count that pays off only up to a ceiling (k1), and a lower TF part for long passages (b). With k1 = 0, BM25 is just word overlap with IDF weights. (Steps 1, 2 and 3)

  2. What is the score built from in word-overlap and in vector retrieval?

    Show the answer

    In BM25 the score comes only from terms that appear exactly in both the query and the passage, a dot product of long sparse vectors. In vector retrieval an encoder turns each text into a short dense vector, and the score is the dot product of those vectors. (Steps 5 and 9)

Read the BM25 slide

  1. What is the IDF(qi) in the yellow box for hour? What are the numbers in the green and pink boxes?

    Show the answer

    Green is N = 6, and pink is n(hour) = 1, because hour appears only in the queue log. IDF = ln((6 − 1 + 0.5) ÷ (1 + 0.5)) = ln(5.5 ÷ 1.5) = 1.299. (Steps 1 and 6)

  2. What is the blue box's |D| ÷ avgdl for the queue log, and what does the whole bracket around it come to?

    Show the answer

Read the slide with the graphs

  1. Why does the blue line in the left graph cross 0 exactly at 50%?

    Show the answer

    At 50%, n = N ÷ 2, so the top, N − n + 0.5, equals the bottom, n + 0.5. The ratio is 1, and ln 1 = 0. (Steps 1 and 7)

  2. Why does the green dashed line never go below 0, and where are the two lines at 100%?

    Show the answer

    The green line is ln(N ÷ n), and n is never larger than N, so the ratio is at least 1. At 100% it sits at ln 1 = 0. The blue line there falls, by the formula, to ln(0.5 ÷ 10,000.5) = −9.904. (Step 7)

  3. Why do all four lines in the right graph pass through the same point at f = 1? And what height is the k1 = 5 line heading for?

    Show the answer

    At f = 1 the TF part is (k1 + 1) ÷ (1 + k1) = 1, whatever k1 is. The k1 = 5 line is heading for its ceiling of 6, and at f = 20 it is at 4.8. The graph has no length in it, so it shows the factor = 1 case. (Steps 2 and 7)

    Show the answer

3 points from this lesson

  1. BM25 score = each query term's IDF × its TF part, summed over the terms. IDF gives weight to rare terms, k1 makes the count saturate, and b shrinks the count by length.
  2. k1 and b are hyperparameters, picked once for the whole corpus on a dev set. On our query the usual values keep the forum post on top. With b = 0 and k1 = 1.3 the queue log comes out on top, but only because of repetition.
  3. Because of the inverted index, BM25 reads only the lists of the query's terms, so it runs in tens of milliseconds and is reliable on exact terms. It fails on queries in different words, and it does not see whether a passage holds data or an opinion.

New terms in this lesson

Term
One word of the query in search. For example, office and hour in which office hour.
Document frequency, n(q)
How many documents contain term q. For example, n = 1 for milestone and 2 for office.
IDF
ln((N − n + 0.5) ÷ (n + 0.5)), a bigger weight for a rarer term. For example, 1.299 for milestone and 0.588 for office.
TF-IDF
The term's count × ln(N ÷ n), an older method than BM25. For example, N = 6, n = 2, f = 2 gives 2 × ln 3 = 2.20.
Term frequency, f(q, D)
How many times term q appears in document D. For example, office has f = 2 in the queue log.
Saturation and k1
As the count grows, the gain fills up slowly towards a ceiling of k1 + 1. For example, with k1 = 1.2 the TF part for f = 1, 2, 10 is 1.000, 1.375, 1.964.
Length normalization, b and avgdl
Lowering the TF part of documents longer than average, by an amount set by b. For example, with avgdl = 16.17 and b = 0.75, a document twice the average length gets 0.710.
BM25
Each query term's IDF × its TF part, summed over the terms. For example, 2.749 for the forum post and 2.473 for the queue log.
Hyperparameter
A number in a formula or model that training does not change, picked by trying values on a dev set. For example, BM25's k1 and b, which the DPR paper set to 0.9 and 0.4.
Inverted index
For each term, the list of documents that contain it. For example, office → office hours page, queue log.
Sparse and dense vector
A long vector that is mostly zeros, and a short one that is almost full. For example, 55 of the 69 slots in the forum post's vector are 0.
