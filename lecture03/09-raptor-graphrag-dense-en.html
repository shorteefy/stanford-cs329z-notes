Questions spread across a whole document: RAPTOR and GraphRAG

RAPTOR and GraphRAG put summaries into the index next to the chunks, so that answers spread across a whole document can still be found. RAPTOR builds a tree of summaries, and GraphRAG builds a graph of the things in the text and how they relate.

You can see the need with this question: What are all the deadlines? The answer is 4 dates in the course, spread over three passages from lesson 2. The syllabus has the milestone (Week 6) and the final report (Week 10). The project page has the proposal (Oct 9), and the Homework 1 announcement has its release date (Oct 5). The retriever ranks the project page first, so with K = 1 the reader gets 1 of the 4 dates. from my vectors

2Groups of similar passages: clustering

One summary of all 6 passages would be too coarse. A summary of the deadlines and a separate one of office hours are more useful. So before writing summaries, split the passages into groups by topic.

Clustering means splitting vectors into groups so that the vectors in a group are close to each other and far from the other groups. Each group is called a cluster. Here cosine is enough to measure “close”: the nearer the cosine is to 1, the more the two vectors point the same way.

To keep the arithmetic small, use the 2-number vectors from lesson 3. The first number says how much the text is about office hours, and the second how much it is about deadlines. my vectors

Each cluster also has a middle point that can stand in for the whole cluster. The centroid is the average of the cluster's vectors, taken separately for each position. In the deadlines cluster the first number is (0.1 + 0.2 + 0.15) ÷ 3 = 0.15, and the second is (0.9 + 0.8 + 0.85) ÷ 3 = 0.85. So the centroid is [0.15, 0.85], and the office hours cluster's is [0.85, 0.15]. In lesson 10, centroids make search faster.

Some passages sit between two topics. The long FAQ page from lesson 3, [1.6, 1.4], has a cosine of 0.86 with the office hours centroid and 0.78 with the deadlines centroid. RAPTOR can put such a passage into both clusters, so that its content reaches both summaries. This is called soft clustering.

3RAPTOR: a tree of summaries, built bottom up

RAPTOR has an LLM write a summary of each cluster, and stores that summary in the index just like a chunk. The summary is new text, not an average of vectors. So its vector points towards “all the deadlines”, whereas the vector of lesson 7's big chunk got lost in the average.

Summaries can themselves be summarized: the deadlines summary and the office hours summary give one summary of the whole course. A structure with levels like this is called a tree. Each box in the tree is a node. The nodes at the bottom, the actual chunks, are called leaves, and the node at the top is the root.

RAPTOR stands for Recursive Abstractive Processing for Tree-Organized Retrieval, and Sarthi and colleagues at Stanford built it in 2024 from the paper. The tree is built bottom up in five steps:

  1. Embed every chunk.
  2. Cluster the embeddings.
  3. Give each cluster's chunks to an LLM and have it write their summary. The summary is a new node one level up.
  4. Embed the summaries.
  5. Repeat steps 2 to 4 until only a handful of nodes are left at the top.

On the course corpus the tree looks like this. The 6 passages are the leaves, there are 2 summaries above them, and 1 root at the top. my example

The cost comes at indexing time, because every cluster on every level needs one LLM call. An LLM writes the summaries, so it can drop a fine detail or get something wrong. Node B puts the old post's opinion on the same footing as the queue log's data. So the old forum post's problem now sits inside a summary.

5GraphRAG: a graph of things and relations

RAPTOR forms its groups by how close the vectors are. But documents also state relations between things outright: the milestone is due in Week 6, and the TAs run office hours. GraphRAG forms its groups from these relations, and answers questions about the whole corpus from each group's summary.

Any named thing is called an entity: a person, a place, a course, a document or a date. Examples are Project milestone, Week 6, TAs and Room 104. A link between two entities is called a relation, and it is written in three parts: (entity, relation, entity). For example, (Project milestone, due, Week 6).

In GraphRAG an LLM reads every chunk and pulls out these relations. The course corpus gives these 7 rows: my example

Now make every entity a node, and every relation a line between two nodes. These lines are called edges, and a network of nodes and edges is a graph. A graph of entities and relations is called a knowledge graph. From the first row, Project links to three nodes, and each part links to its date.

In some parts of a graph the nodes are tightly linked to each other and only loosely to the rest. Such a part is called a community. Our graph has three communities, and no edge links them to each other. The first holds Project, its three parts and their dates. The second holds Office hours, TAs, Room 104 and Zoom, and the third holds Homework 1 and Oct 5.

Edge and colleagues at Microsoft Research built GraphRAG in 2024 from the paper. It has five steps at indexing time and two at query time:

Indexing timeQuery time
1. Split the documents into chunks.
2. An LLM pulls entities and relations out of every chunk.
3. Build the knowledge graph from them.
4. Find the communities in the graph.
5. An LLM writes a summary of each community.		6. From each community's summary, an LLM writes a partial answer to the question.
7. An LLM combines all the partial answers into one global answer.

For our question, step 6 writes one partial answer per community. The Project community gives three dates, the Homework 1 community gives one, and the office hours community gives none. Step 7 combines them into an answer with all four dates.

In steps 6 and 7 the LLM writes its summary with the question in mind, which is called query-focused summarization. The slide's title uses exactly this term. RAPTOR's summaries are written before any question arrives, while GraphRAG's final summary is written when the question arrives.

GraphRAG pays off on global questions about the whole corpus, where top-K passages do not work at all. The cost is high: LLM calls for every chunk and every community at indexing time, and one call per community at query time too. If the LLM extracts a wrong entity or relation, the mistake settles in the graph and reaches that community's summary.

6The maths of RAPTOR's clustering: the Gaussian mixture and soft membership

In step 2 the clusters were formed by hand from cosines, and the FAQ page went into both. RAPTOR makes this decision with a probability model called a Gaussian mixture model (GMM) from the paper. The model gives every passage a probability for every cluster, and soft clustering runs on that probability. We build it in four moves: distance, the bell curve of one cluster, the mixture of several curves, and membership.

Distance: how far from the centroid

A GMM looks at instead of cosine. The distance between two vectors is the length of their difference: square each number of the difference, add them up, and take the square root. The old forum post is [0.8, 0.15], and the office hours centroid is [0.85, 0.15]. The difference is [−0.05, 0], so the distance is √(0.0025 + 0) = 0.05.

From the deadlines centroid [0.15, 0.85] the difference is [0.65, −0.7], and the distance is √(0.4225 + 0.49) = √0.9125 = 0.955. So the forum post sits very close to the office hours centroid, and about 19 times as far from the deadlines one.

One cluster: a bell curve

A GMM treats each cluster as a bell curve that is highest at the centroid and falls quickly as you move away. This curve is called a Gaussian. Its height follows e−d²/(2σ²), where d is the distance from the centroid. σ (sigma) is the cluster's spread, how far its points usually lie from the centroid, and it is called the .

A negative power of e is smaller than 1, because e−a = 1 ÷ ea. In lesson 4, (−1) = 1 ÷ 2.718 = 0.368. So the further the distance, the faster the height falls:

For data with one number, the full Gaussian formula is this, where x is the point and μ (mu) is the middle of the curve:

𝒩(x; μ, σ) = 1σ√(2π) e−(x − μ)²/(2σ²)

The factor 1 ÷ (σ√(2π)) in front only makes the total area under the curve equal 1. With μ = 0 and σ = 1, the height is 0.399 at x = 0, 0.242 at x = 1 and 0.054 at x = 2. Check: 0.242 ÷ 0.399 = 0.607, the second row of the table. This height is not a probability. It is called a density, and for a narrow curve it can go above 1.

For a vector with two numbers, (x − μ)² is replaced by d², the squared distance from the centroid, and the factor in front becomes 1 ÷ (2πσ²). Here I assume the spread is the same in every direction, and that both clusters have σ = 0.25 my choice of σ. Then 2σ² = 0.125, and the factor in front is 1 ÷ (2π × 0.0625) = 2.546.

Several clusters: the mixture

A Gaussian mixture is a weighted sum of several bell curves. Each cluster k has a weight πk, the share of all passages that belong to it. Our 6 passages split 3 and 3, so both weights are 0.5, and the height of the mixture at a point x is:

P(x) = 0.5 × 𝒩office hours(x) + 0.5 × 𝒩deadlines(x)

This is the from lesson 4: each case's weight times that case's probability, then add them all, as in 0.5 × 0.8 + 0.5 × 0.1 = 0.45. There the cases were days, and here the cases are clusters. The paper writes the same thing for K clusters:

P(x) = ∑k=1K πk 𝒩(x; μk, Σk)

In our example K = 2, both πk are 0.5, and the μk are the two centroids, [0.85, 0.15] and [0.15, 0.85]. Here Σk is not the summation sign. It is the spread of cluster k, which can differ from direction to direction. In our simplification it is σ² = 0.0625 in every direction.

Soft membership: a probability for each cluster

Now take a new passage that mixes both topics: In milestone week, the Thursday office hours will run longer. Its vector is [0.6, 0.4] my passage. Membership is the probability that this passage came from cluster k. To get it, multiply the cluster's height by its weight, and divide by the total height P(x). In lesson 4, p(Tuesday | full) = 0.40 ÷ 0.45 = 0.89 came out exactly this way.

  1. Squared distances: from the office hours centroid, (0.6 − 0.85)² + (0.4 − 0.15)² = 0.0625 + 0.0625 = 0.125, and from the deadlines one, (0.6 − 0.15)² + (0.4 − 0.85)² = 0.2025 + 0.2025 = 0.405.
  2. Heights: e−0.125/0.125 = e−1 = 0.368, and e−0.405/0.125 = e−3.24 = 0.0392. Multiplying by 2.546 gives 𝒩office hours = 0.937 and 𝒩deadlines = 0.0997.
  3. Times the weights: 0.5 × 0.937 = 0.468 and 0.5 × 0.0997 = 0.0499. The sum P(x) is 0.518.
  4. Membership: office hours 0.468 ÷ 0.518 = 0.904, and deadlines 0.0499 ÷ 0.518 = 0.096. The two add up to 1.
p(k | x) = πk 𝒩(x; μk, Σk)∑j πj 𝒩(x; μj, Σj)

The top is the 0.468 from step 3, and the bottom is the sum 0.518. The 2.546 and the 0.5 appear on both top and bottom, so they cancel in the division. What remains is just the of e−1 and e−3.24: 0.368 ÷ (0.368 + 0.0392) = 0.904.

In RAPTOR's code a passage joins every cluster where its membership is above 0.1 from the RAPTOR code. Our passage's deadlines membership is 0.096, which is below 0.1. So it joins only the office hours cluster.

Its squared distance from both centroids is (0.35)² + (0.35)² = 0.245, so both heights are the same: e−1.96 = 0.141. The memberships are 0.5 and 0.5, both above 0.1, so the chunk joins both clusters. (Steps 2 and 6)

I chose σ = 0.25 so the numbers stay readable. A real GMM works out μ, σ and π from the data itself, using a method called Expectation-Maximization (EM) from the paper. EM first computes every passage's memberships, then uses them to recompute the centroids, spreads and weights. It alternates these two moves until the numbers settle.

On our 6 points such a fit gives about σ = 0.04, because the points in each cluster are very close together my arithmetic. With σ that small, most memberships are almost exactly 0 or 1, and only passages right in the middle get split.

How many clusters: choosing with BIC

A GMM has to be told how many clusters there are. RAPTOR picks the count with BIC (the Bayesian Information Criterion): try several counts and keep the one with the smallest BIC from the paper. The paper's formula is:

BIC = ln(N) · k − 2 ln(L̂)

N is the number of passages, here 6, and ln 6 = 1.792. L̂ is the of the best fit, the product of every passage's P(x). Lesson 4's rule ln(a × b) = ln a + ln b turns that product into a sum. k is the number of model parameters, the numbers the fit decides.

In our simplified model each centroid has 2 numbers, and all clusters share one σ. K clusters have K − 1 weights, because the last weight is fixed by the others. my arithmetic

The K = 2 row works out like this. In each passage's P(x) the other cluster's part is almost 0, so ln P(x) = ln 0.5 + ln 𝒩. The fit's σ² is 0.001667, and the squared distances add up to 0.02.

  1. Weights: 6 × ln 0.5 = 6 × (−0.693) = −4.159.
  2. Factor in front: 1 ÷ (2π × 0.001667) = 95.5, and 6 × ln 95.5 = 6 × 4.559 = 27.354.
  3. Distances: −0.02 ÷ (2 × 0.001667) = −0.02 ÷ 0.003333 = −6.0.
  4. Sum: −4.159 + 27.354 − 6.0 = 17.195, or 17.20. Then BIC = 1.792 × 6 − 2 × 17.195 = 10.75 − 34.39 = −23.64.

ln L̂ is positive here because the densities of narrow curves are above 1. At K = 3, ln L̂ rises only from 17.20 to 18.12, but the parameter penalty grows from 10.75 to 16.13. So BIC is smallest at K = 2, and RAPTOR would pick that count.

With 768 numbers the nearest and the farthest point are almost equally far away, so “close” loses its meaning. That is why RAPTOR runs UMAP before clustering. UMAP turns each vector into one with far fewer numbers, and tries to keep points that were close before close in the new vectors. In RAPTOR's code the new vectors have 10 numbers from the RAPTOR code.

UMAP's n_neighbors sets how many neighbours each point looks at to decide its place. A large n_neighbors keeps the rough shape of the whole data, and a small one keeps the fine detail nearby. RAPTOR first forms rough global clusters, then local clusters inside each global one. If a cluster's text is longer than the summary model's token limit, RAPTOR clusters again inside it. from the paper

7The RAPTOR paper: two ways to search the tree, the setup and the results

Tree traversal and the collapsed tree

The paper tries two ways of searching the tree from the paper. Tree traversal works top down. From the top level, pick the k nodes closest to the question, then the k closest among only their children, and so on down to the leaves. The collapsed tree turns the whole tree into one list and sorts all nodes by cosine. It then adds nodes from the top until a token limit is full.

On our tree, traversal with k = 1 runs like this for the all-deadlines question. The root R is alone, so traversal starts there. Among its children, A (1.00) beats B (0.62). Among A's children, the project page (0.66) beats Homework (0.61) and the syllabus (0.60). The context is R, A and the project page.

The collapsed tree needs each node's token count, and we set the limit at 100 tokens: my token counts

The difference shows on a detail question. On the milestone question, traversal takes one node per level: R (0.33), A (0.52), and then the syllabus (0.99). The collapsed tree puts the syllabus first. In traversal the mix of levels is the same for every question, while the collapsed tree picks the level to suit the question. The paper tried both on 20 QASPER stories, and the collapsed tree did better in every setting.

For its main experiments RAPTOR runs the collapsed tree with a limit of 2,000 tokens, which is about the top 20 nodes. The UnifiedQA reader reads only 512 tokens at a time, so it gets 400 tokens of context. The baselines get the same number of tokens. from the paper

How the tree was built

The paper's setup: from the paper

  • Chunks are 100 tokens long. If a sentence would cross the 100-token limit, the whole sentence moves to the next chunk instead of being cut.
  • Embeddings come from SBERT, a BERT-based encoder (multi-qa-mpnet-base-cos-v1).
  • gpt-3.5-turbo writes the summaries, with the prompt Write a summary of the following, including as many key details as possible.
  • A parent has 6.7 children on average, and a summary is about 0.28 times as long as its children's text combined, so 72% shorter.
  • The authors checked 150 nodes by hand and found a minor hallucination in 6 of them, which is 4%. These errors did not spread to the summaries above, and showed no effect on the answers.
  • Both the tokens and the time needed to build the tree grow in a straight line with the document's length.

Results on three datasets

The paper measures on three datasets. NarrativeQA has questions about books and movie scripts, 1,572 documents in all. QASPER has 5,049 questions about 1,585 NLP papers, and QuALITY has multiple-choice questions about passages of about 5,000 tokens. from the paper

QASPER is scored with F1, which compares the words of the reader's answer with the words of the right answer. Say the answer has 4 words, the right answer has 5, and 3 words appear in both my numbers. is 3 ÷ 4 = 0.75, and is 3 ÷ 5 = 0.6. F1 = 2 × 0.75 × 0.6 ÷ (0.75 + 0.6) = 0.9 ÷ 1.35 = 0.667, written as a percentage.

8The GraphRAG paper: building the graph, the map-reduce answer and the results

From chunks to a graph

GraphRAG splits documents into chunks of 600 tokens with a 100-token from the paper. So each new chunk brings 500 new tokens. Chunk size is a trade-off: larger chunks need fewer LLM calls, but the LLM misses entities in them. On HotPotQA data, GPT-4 found almost twice as many entity references in 600-token chunks as in 2,400-token chunks.

So GraphRAG gives the LLM a second pass, called gleaning. After extraction, GraphRAG asks the LLM a yes-or-no question: were any entities missed? If yes, the next prompt says many entities were missed, and the LLM extracts more. GraphRAG repeats this up to a set number of times. from the paper

The LLM also writes a short description for every entity and relation. It extracts claims as well, factual statements such as dates or events. When the same entity comes out of several chunks, it becomes one node, and the LLM summarizes its descriptions. The more times a relation is extracted, the higher its edge weight. If (Office hours, run by, TAs) came out of two passages, that edge would have weight 2.

Levels of communities: Leiden

To find communities, GraphRAG runs the Leiden algorithm from the paper. Leiden splits a graph into parts with many edges inside and few edges between them. GraphRAG runs it again and again: first communities, then sub-communities inside each community, until nothing can be split further.

This gives levels of communities: C0 is the top level, with the fewest communities, and C3 the bottom, with the most. On each level every node is in exactly one community. Here GraphRAG differs from RAPTOR, where one passage could sit in two clusters. Our graph would have three communities at level 0. At a lower level the Project community could split into a proposal part and a milestone part.

When writing a community's summary, the LLM's token limit can fill up, so GraphRAG puts the important parts first. A node's degree is the number of edges on it, and an edge's importance is the sum of its two nodes' degrees. In our graph Project has degree 3 and Proposal has degree 2. So (Project, has part, Proposal) has importance 3 + 2 = 5, and (Proposal, due, Oct 9) has 2 + 1 = 3. The edges with 5 go into the summary first.

When a question arrives: map-reduce

At query time GraphRAG writes its answer with map-reduce. Map means doing the same job on each piece separately, and reduce means combining all the results. In the paper this takes three steps: from the paper

  1. Shuffle the community summaries of the chosen level, and split them into pieces of a fixed number of tokens.
  2. Map: from each piece the LLM writes a partial answer. It also gives a score from 0 to 100 for how helpful the answer is, and parts that score 0 are dropped.
  3. Reduce: the remaining parts are added in score order until the context window is full, and the LLM writes the final answer from them.

For our question, the Project part scores 95, the Homework 1 part 80, and the office hours part 0 my scores. The office hours part is dropped, and the reduce step reads the Project part, then the Homework 1 part, and writes all four dates. The paper used an 8k-token window for the summaries, the partial answers and the final answer.

Results from an LLM judge

Global questions have no single right answer, so the paper had an LLM write the questions too. For each dataset the LLM imagined 5 kinds of users, and 5 tasks for each user. Each task got 5 questions, so 5 × 5 × 5 = 125 questions. An example from a tech journalist: Which episodes deal primarily with tech policy and government regulation? from the paper

The paper used two datasets. The podcast transcripts were 1,669 chunks of 600 tokens (about 1 million tokens), and the news articles 3,197 chunks (about 1.7 million tokens). The graph built from the podcasts had 8,564 nodes and 20,691 edges, and indexing it with gpt-4-turbo took 281 minutes. from the paper

A second LLM then acted as the judge. It got a question and two systems' answers, and said for each criterion which answer was better, or that they were equal. There were four criteria: comprehensiveness (how much it covers), diversity (how many different angles), empowerment (how well the reader can understand and decide), and directness (how direct the answer is). The paper kept directness on purpose, as a check on the other results. It is the opposite of comprehensiveness, so no method can win on all four criteria.

Each pair was compared 5 times on every question, which gives 125 × 5 = 625 judgments. The win rate is the percentage of these in which the judge preferred one system, and 50% means a tie. A 72% win rate, for example, means about 0.72 × 625 = 450 judgments my arithmetic.

The paper compared six methods: the four community levels C0 to C3, TS and SS. TS runs the same map-reduce directly on the chunks, without a graph, and SS is ordinary vector RAG, which takes the closest chunks. On comprehensiveness, the global methods (the graph levels and TS) had win rates over SS of 72% to 83% on the podcasts and 72% to 80% on the news. On diversity they were 75% to 82% and 62% to 71%. On directness SS won, as expected. from the paper

9The whole GraphRAG family: the survey by Han and colleagues

Edge's GraphRAG is one system, but the name is now also used for a whole family. The 2025 survey by Han and colleagues calls any RAG whose data source is a graph a GraphRAG from the paper. Stanford's last GraphRAG slide comes from this survey, and you read it in step 10.

The survey splits every GraphRAG into five parts, and these are the five boxes in the middle of the slide. For our all-deadlines question they look like this: my course example

PartJobIn our course
Query processorMake the question searchableTurn “deadlines” into entities: Proposal, Project milestone, Final report, Homework 1
RetrieverFetch the useful part of the graphWalk the due and released edges from these nodes to fetch the dates
Data sourceThe graph itselfThe knowledge graph from step 5
OrganizerTrim and order what was fetchedWrite the four relations as four lines of text
GeneratorWrite the answerThe LLM, which writes the four dates

The left part of the slide shows ordinary RAG data: text and images. According to the survey, text is a line of tokens, and an image is a grid of small patches. Different sentences share the same tokens. Graph data has no shared unit like that, and its structure differs from domain to domain. So each domain needs its own GraphRAG design. from the paper

The right part of the slide shows kinds of graphs:

  • What nodes are: entities, research papers, products or customers. For example, citation edges between papers, or purchase edges between customers and products.
  • Graph: one row of a knowledge graph, (California, State, USA), which has the same shape as our (Project milestone, due, Week 6). A protein's structure is also a graph.
  • Cellular complex: besides edges, it has parts that treat several nodes as one unit. Examples are the whole ring of benzene, or the four members of a club who are all linked to each other.
  • Homophily and heterophily: in homophily, linked nodes are alike, like friends in a social network. In heterophily, linked nodes are of different kinds, as in a dating network.
  • Proximity similar and role similar: in the slide's tree, 2 and 4 are alike because they are linked. 2 and 3 are not linked, but they hold the same position: both are children of 1, and both have two children. In our graph, Project milestone and Final report are role similar, because both are parts of Project and both have a due date.
  • 6-cycle and 4-cycle: benzene has a ring of 6 nodes, and a street grid has rings of 4 nodes. In drug design or city planning, the retriever has to recognize such shapes.

The survey lists 10 such domains: knowledge, document, scientific, social, planning and reasoning, tabular, infrastructure, biological, scene and random graphs. Edge's GraphRAG belongs to the document graph domain. from the paper

3 points from this lesson

  1. A thematic question's answer is not in any single chunk. Every chunk gets a low cosine, raising K is not enough, and a big chunk averages everything away.
  2. RAPTOR uses a GMM to give each chunk a membership for every cluster, and soft clustering lets a chunk sit in more than one cluster. It builds the tree from cluster summaries and searches every level with the collapsed tree. With GPT-4 it reaches 82.6% accuracy on QuALITY, 20.3 points above the previous best.
  3. GraphRAG has an LLM build a knowledge graph, finds levels of communities with Leiden, and writes a global answer with map-reduce. Its comprehensiveness win rate over vector RAG is 72% to 83%, and its top level needs only 2.3% to 2.6% of the tokens of the raw chunks.

New terms in this lesson

Detail question and thematic question
The answer is written in one place, or it is built from several passages. For example, “When is the milestone due?” and “What are all the deadlines?”.
Clustering
Splitting vectors into groups so that the vectors in a group are close to each other. For example, the syllabus [0.1, 0.9] and the project page [0.2, 0.8] have cosine 0.99, so they share a group.
Cluster and centroid
A cluster is a group, and its centroid is the average of its vectors. For example, the centroid of [0.1, 0.9], [0.2, 0.8] and [0.15, 0.85] is [0.15, 0.85].
Soft clustering
Putting one passage into more than one cluster. For example, the FAQ page, whose cosine is high with both centroids (0.86 and 0.78).
RAPTOR
Building a tree bottom up from summaries of clusters, and searching every level. For example, the all-deadlines question matches summary A.
Tree, node, leaf, root
A structure with levels: a node is a box, a leaf is a node at the bottom, and the root is the node at the top. For example, 2 summaries above 6 chunks (the leaves), and 1 root above them.
Entity
Any named thing: a person, a place, a course, a document or a date. For example, Week 6, TAs, Room 104.
Relation
A link between two entities. For example, (Project milestone, due, Week 6).
Knowledge graph and edge
A network of entities (nodes) and relations (edges). For example, an edge labelled “due” between the milestone and Week 6.
Community
A tightly linked part of a graph. For example, Office hours, TAs, Room 104 and Zoom.
GraphRAG
Answering global questions from a knowledge graph, its communities and their summaries. For example, the all-deadlines answer, built from the Project and Homework 1 communities.
Query-focused summarization
Writing a summary with the question in mind. For example, asking for “all the deadlines” makes the Project community's summary give only the dates.
Gaussian mixture model (GMM)
A weighted sum of bell curves: P(x) = ∑ πk 𝒩k(x). It gives every passage a membership for every cluster. For example, [0.6, 0.4] gets 0.904 for office hours and 0.096 for deadlines.
BIC
ln(N) · k − 2 ln(L̂): choosing the number of clusters by weighing the fit's likelihood against its parameter count, where the smallest BIC wins. For example, on 6 passages K = 2 gives −23.64 and K = 3 gives −20.11.
UMAP
Turning vectors into ones with fewer numbers while keeping nearby points nearby. For example, RAPTOR's code makes every vector 10 numbers long.
Tree traversal and collapsed tree
Picking the top k on each level from the top down, or sorting all nodes in one list and taking them up to a token limit. For example, for the all-deadlines question the collapsed tree takes 5 nodes within 100 tokens, A first.
F1
A score that combines precision and recall, 2PR ÷ (P + R). For example, P = 0.75 and R = 0.6 give F1 = 0.667.
Leiden and degree
Leiden splits a graph into communities with many edges inside. A node's degree is its number of edges. For example, Project has degree 3.
Map-reduce
Doing the same job on each piece separately (map), then combining the results (reduce). For example, a partial answer and a score from each community, then a final answer in score order.
Win rate
The percentage of judgments that favour one of two systems, where 50% means a tie. For example, about 450 of 625 judgments, or 72%.
