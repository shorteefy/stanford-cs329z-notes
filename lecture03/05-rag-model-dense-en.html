The RAG model: retriever and generator together

RAG is a model made of two models. The retriever brings passages for a question and gives each one a weight, and the generator writes the answer with each passage. Both are trained together with one loss, and that training needs only question-answer pairs. RAG stands for Retrieval-Augmented Generation, a model published by Lewis and colleagues in 2020.

The need for it showed up in the last question of lesson 4. There the retriever gave the old forum post a weight of 0.731 and the queue log only 0.269. The generator's numbers stayed the same, yet the probability of the right answer, Thursday 9am, fell from 0.685 to 0.315. The mistake is the retriever's, but the training data holds only questions and answers. Nothing in it says the answer came from the queue log, and RAG teaches the retriever anyway.

1RAG's two models: retriever and generator

RAG contains two models. The retriever pη(z|x) gives every passage z a weight, and the generator pθ(y|x, z) reads the question x and one passage and writes the answer y. These are the same two probabilities that appeared in the formula of lesson 4. The small η (eta) and θ (theta) written underneath name the two models' , the numbers that training changes.

The retriever: DPR

RAG's retriever is a model called DPR, whose own training comes in lesson 12. It has two , both built from BERT, an encoder model Google released in 2018. The query encoder makes the question's vector q(x), and the passage encoder makes a vector d(z) for every passage z.

A passage's score is the of these two vectors, written d(z)⊤q(x). Then turns the scores into weights that add up to 1. In lesson 4, scores 1.0 and 0.0 became weights 0.731 and 0.269. Stanford's slide writes the same thing like this:

pη(z|x) ∝ exp(d(z)⊤q(x))

Here means: take each passage's exp, then divide them all by one and the same number so that they add up to 1.

There are a great many passages. In the paper, Wikipedia is cut into about 21 million passages of 100 words each. from the paper Their vectors are built once, before any question arrives, and kept in an . When a question arrives, only the query encoder runs, and then a search pulls out the passages with the K largest dot products. This search is called MIPS, maximum inner product search, because inner product is another name for the dot product.

The generator: BART

The generator reads the question and one passage and writes the answer, one token at a time, like the LLM in lesson 1. RAG's generator is , an encoder-decoder model. Its encoder reads the input text and turns it into vectors, and its decoder looks at those vectors while it writes the answer token by token.

BART reads a single text, so the question and the passage are joined end to end into one text. Joining two texts like this is called concatenating them. With the queue log, the input and the output look like this: my example

Input: question: Which office hour is least crowded? passage: Queue log, last 3 weeks: Tuesday 4pm office hour averaged 14 students waiting; Thursday 9am office hour averaged 3.
Output: Thursday 9am

This design has a cost. The generator runs separately with each passage, so with K = 5 it runs 5 times for one question.

2Marginalization: no passage label needed

RAG treats the passage as a hidden choice, and gets the answer's probability as a weighted sum over all the passages it brought. A hidden choice like this is called a latent variable. The answer came from some passage, but the data does not say which one.

Teaching the retriever directly would need the right passage written next to every question. Nobody picks passages by hand for millions of questions. from lesson 4 is the method for exactly this situation: multiply each case's probability by its weight, then add everything up. There it was 0.5 × 0.8 + 0.5 × 0.1 = 0.45.

Now take the question Which office hour is least crowded? The retriever brought 3 passages, so K = 3, and their scores are 1.8, 1.1 and 0.0. In softmax, e1.8 = 6.05, e1.1 = 3.00 and e0 = 1, which add up to 10.05. So the queue log's weight is 6.05 ÷ 10.05 = 0.60, and the other two get 0.30 and 0.10 the same way. my numbers

With each passage, the generator gives Thursday 9am a probability. Each row multiplies the weight by the generator's probability:

For Wednesday afternoon the generator's numbers are 0.05, 0.2 and 0.8, because the forum post talks about midweek afternoons. Its sum is 0.6 × 0.05 + 0.3 × 0.2 + 0.1 × 0.8 = 0.03 + 0.06 + 0.08 = 0.17. So RAG picks Thursday 9am, 0.70 against 0.17.

In symbols, the same arithmetic reads:

p(y|x) ≈ ∑z ∈ top-K pη(z|x) · pθ(y|x, z)

pη(z|x) are the weights (0.6, 0.3, 0.1), pθ(y|x, z) are the generator's numbers (0.9, 0.5, 0.1), and ∑ adds up their products (0.70). The sum runs only over the top-K passages, not over the other 21 million, which is why the formula has instead of “=”.

Nowhere in this calculation does it say that the right passage is the queue log. The formula needs only the question x and the answer y, and the passage stays hidden inside the sum. So question-answer pairs are enough to train RAG.

3RAG-Sequence and RAG-Token: when the weighted sum happens

RAG-Sequence writes the whole answer with each passage, then takes the weighted sum. RAG-Token takes a separate weighted sum at every token of the answer. So far the whole answer was one piece, but the generator writes it one token at a time. When different parts of the answer sit in different passages, the two methods give different results.

The probability of a whole answer is the product of its tokens' probabilities, which is the from lesson 4. If Thursday has probability 0.9, and then Zoom has 0.2, the whole answer has 0.9 × 0.2 = 0.18.

Now take the question Least crowded office hour, and where is it? The answer has two tokens: Thursday and Zoom. The queue log says Thursday is the quietest but not where it is held. The office hours page says Thursday 9am on Zoom but not which slot is quiet. my numbers

PassageWeightp(Thursday | x, z)p(Zoom | x, z, Thursday)
Queue log0.60.90.2
Office hours page0.40.50.9

RAG-Sequence first writes the whole answer with each passage, then takes the weighted sum:

  1. Whole answer with the queue log: 0.9 × 0.2 = 0.18.
  2. Whole answer with the office hours page: 0.5 × 0.9 = 0.45.
  3. Weighted sum: 0.6 × 0.18 + 0.4 × 0.45 = 0.108 + 0.180 = 0.288.

The slide puts it this way: pick one passage, generate the whole answer with it.

RAG-Token first takes the weighted sum at each token, then multiplies the tokens:

  1. Thursday: 0.6 × 0.9 + 0.4 × 0.5 = 0.54 + 0.20 = 0.74.
  2. Zoom: 0.6 × 0.2 + 0.4 × 0.9 = 0.12 + 0.36 = 0.48.
  3. Product: 0.74 × 0.48 = 0.355.

On the slide this is: re-pick the passage at every token of the answer.

RAG-Token gets the higher probability, 0.355 against 0.288. It can lean on the queue log for Thursday and on the office hours page for Zoom. RAG-Sequence has to write the whole answer from a single passage, and no single passage knows both facts.

The two formulas hold the same pieces, with ∑ and in swapped places. ∏ means multiply everything over the range written below it, just as ∑ means add everything. Here yi is the i-th token of the answer, and y<i is all the tokens before it.

RAG-Sequence: p(y|x) ≈ ∑z p(z|x) ∏i p(yi | x, z, y<i)
RAG-Token: p(y|x) ≈ ∏i ∑z p(z|x) p(yi | x, z, y<i)

In RAG-Sequence the ∏ is inside: first the tokens are multiplied for each passage (0.18 and 0.45), then comes the ∑. In RAG-Token the ∑ is inside: first the passages are added for each token (0.74 and 0.48), then comes the ∏. In the results of step 5 the two methods come out almost equal.

4Training the retriever and the generator with one loss

RAG's training makes −ln of the right answer's probability small, and this single loss changes both the retriever and the generator. Training all the parts together with one loss is called end-to-end training. The data holds only question-answer pairs, yet this loss is how the retriever learns which passage deserves more weight.

The is the one from lesson 4: −ln of the right answer's probability p. In step 2, Thursday 9am got 0.70, so the loss is −ln 0.70 = 0.357. Over all training examples j, these losses add up:

∑j − log p(yj | xj)

The slide calls this the . It is “marginal” because p(y|x) itself comes from marginalization over passages, and the slide's log is this ln.

The reason lies in the weighted average. p(y|x) is a weighted average of the generator's numbers 0.9, 0.5 and 0.1, meaning each number times its weight, all added up. A weighted average always lies between the smallest and the largest number, and it moves toward whichever number gains weight.

Set the queue log aside for a moment. The office hours page and the forum post have weights in the ratio 3 : 1, that is 0.75 and 0.25 between them. Their average is 0.75 × 0.5 + 0.25 × 0.1 = 0.4. Which parts are trained

Training nudges every parameter a little in the direction that lowers the loss, and the gives that direction. Working backwards from the loss to get every parameter's gradient is called backpropagation, or backprop for short. In RAG the gradient reaches the generator's parameters θ and, inside the retriever, the query encoder.

The passage encoder and the index stay fixed. Changing the passage encoder would mean rebuilding the vectors of 21 million passages and then the index. from the paper So training improves things only from the question's side. It changes the query encoder so that q(x) gets larger dot products with the useful passages. If a passage's vector was built badly, training cannot fix it.

The dashed arrow at the top of the slide in step 1 says exactly this: End-to-End Backprop through q and pθ. The arrow reaches q and pθ, not the d(z) in the document index.

6The maths of the training rule: the loss's rate on a score

The loss's rate on a score tells you how much the loss changes when that score rises a little. Training moves every score against its rate. The rule in step 4 gave only the direction. The rate also tells you how hard each score gets pushed.

A rate: change the input a little, then divide

Finding a rate is simple. Change the input a tiny amount, see how much the output changed, and divide that change by the change in the input. This step uses the exact weights 0.602, 0.299 and 0.099, so the total probability is 0.701 and the loss is 0.355. Now raise one score at a time by 0.01: my arithmetic

Score raised by 0.01Change in lossChange ÷ 0.01
Queue log−0.00171−0.171
Office hours page+0.00086+0.086
Forum post+0.00086+0.086

Take an even smaller change than 0.01, and these rates settle at −0.171, +0.086 and +0.085. The number a rate settles at is written ∂𝓛/∂s. The ∂ means that only this one score changed and everything else stayed the same, and such a rate is called a . The minus sign means the loss falls when the queue log's score rises.

The posterior: each passage's share after seeing the answer

These rates come straight out of the table in step 2. Its product column held each passage's share of the total: 0.54, 0.15 and 0.01, out of 0.70. Divide each share by the total: 0.54 ÷ 0.70 = 0.771, 0.15 ÷ 0.70 = 0.214 and 0.01 ÷ 0.70 = 0.014. With the exact weights the same numbers come out as 0.773, 0.213 and 0.014.

These weights after seeing the answer are called the posterior, written p(z|x, y). The retriever's own weights pη(z|x) come before seeing the answer, and they are called the prior. The prior looks only at the question, while the posterior also looks at how easily the right answer comes out with each passage.

p(z|x, y) = pη(z|x) · pθ(y|x, z)p(y|x)

For the queue log the top is 0.602 × 0.9 = 0.542 and the bottom is 0.701, so the posterior is 0.773. p(z|x, y) and pθ(y|x, z) are different things, just as p(full | Tuesday) and p(Tuesday | full) were different in lesson 4.

Now look at the rates: −0.171 = 0.602 − 0.773, +0.086 = 0.299 − 0.213, and +0.085 = 0.099 − 0.014. The loss's rate on each score is exactly prior − posterior.

∂𝓛 / ∂sz = pη(z|x) − p(z|x, y)

The rule from step 4 is hidden in this. Posterior ÷ prior = pθ(y|x, z) ÷ p(y|x). So the posterior is larger than the prior exactly when the generator's number with that passage is larger than the total. For the queue log, 0.9 ÷ 0.701 = 1.284, and indeed 0.773 ÷ 0.602 = 1.284.

Where this rate comes from, in five steps

Write the generator's numbers as az: 0.9, 0.5 and 0.1. Lesson 4's rule for ln and three rules about rates are enough to reach the formula, and every step comes with a check in numbers:

  1. Split the loss into two parts. A softmax weight is es ÷ Z, where Z = e1.8 + e1.1 + e0 = 10.054. So p(y|x) = u ÷ Z, where u = 6.050 × 0.9 + 3.004 × 0.5 + 1 × 0.1 = 7.047. Apply the rule ln(a ÷ b) = ln a − ln b from lesson 4, and 𝓛 = −ln u + ln Z. Check: −1.953 + 2.308 = 0.355.
  2. The rate of es is es itself. Check: (e1.81 − e1.80) ÷ 0.01 = 6.08, about e1.8 = 6.05.
  3. The rate of ln u is 1 ÷ u. Check: (ln 7.057 − ln 7.047) ÷ 0.01 = 0.1418, and 1 ÷ 7.047 = 0.1419.
  4. Linked rates multiply. This is the for rates. When the queue log's score changes, only the term e1.8 × 0.9 inside u changes, at the rate 5.445. When u changes, ln u changes by 1 ÷ 7.047 times as much. So the rate of ln u is 5.445 ÷ 7.047 = 0.773, the queue log's posterior. In the same way the rate of ln Z is 6.05 ÷ 10.054 = 0.602, its prior.
  5. Add the two parts. In 𝓛 = −ln u + ln Z, the queue log's rate is −0.773 + 0.602 = −0.171. The same arithmetic gives prior − posterior for every passage.

One training step on the scores

Training moves each score against its rate. Take a step size of 1, so subtract each score's rate from it: 1.8 + 0.171 = 1.971, 1.1 − 0.086 = 1.014, and 0.0 − 0.085 = −0.085. The new weights are 0.661, 0.254 and 0.085, the total rises from 0.701 to 0.731, and the loss falls from 0.355 to 0.314. my arithmetic

So training pulls the retriever's weights toward the posterior: the queue log's weight went from 0.602 to 0.661, toward its posterior of 0.773. Real training takes this step on parameters, not on scores, which comes in step 7.

In the broken retriever (scores 0, 0.7 and 1.9), the priors are 0.103, 0.208 and 0.689, and the products 0.093, 0.104 and 0.069, which add up to 0.266. The posteriors come out as 0.349, 0.391 and 0.260, so the rates are −0.246, −0.183 and +0.430. The queue log and the office hours page both go up, and the forum post gets the biggest push down.

7One loss, two models: rates for the generator and the query encoder

The same loss gives rates to the generator and to the query encoder, and the posterior shows up in both. In step 6 everything happened on the scores. Now see how these rates reach the parameters of both models, and why they do not reach the passage encoder.

The rate for the generator: a push as large as the posterior

The generator's number az is the product of its tokens' probabilities, and by the rule from lesson 4, ln az is the sum of their lns. So a rate on ln az passes straight to every token, and that is the rate to look at. Raise each ln az by 0.01, which means multiplying az by e0.01 = 1.01005: my arithmetic

Which generator numberRate, from a 0.01 changePosterior
0.9, with the queue log−0.7740.773
0.5, with the office hours page−0.2140.213
0.1, with the forum post−0.0140.014

The loss's rate on each of the generator's ln az is exactly −posterior. The reason is the same as in step 6. When ln az changes, the term esz az inside u changes at that same rate, and the rate of ln u is 1 ÷ u. Their product esz az ÷ u is the posterior, and the loss holds −ln u, hence the minus sign. Z does not contain az, so that part does not change.

This means the generator learns most to write the right answer with the passage whose posterior is largest. Writing Thursday 9am with the queue log gets a push of 0.773, and with the forum post only 0.014. So the generator does not learn to write Thursday 9am after reading the forum post, which does not contain that fact at all. my reading of the arithmetic, not on the slides

The rate on the query vector, and one step on it

In the retriever the score is d(z)⊤q(x), and training controls only q(x). Now treat q(x) as a real vector with 2 numbers, and give each passage a vector of 2 numbers too. The office hours page keeps its vector from lesson 3. my vectors

If the first number of q(x), q1, rises by 0.01, every score rises by that passage's first number × 0.01. The reason is that in the dot product q1 multiplies only the first numbers. So a score's rate with respect to q1 is the first number of that passage's vector. With the chain rule, the loss's rate with respect to q1 works out like this:

  1. Queue log: score rate −0.171, times its first number 1.0, gives −0.171.
  2. Office hours page: 0.086 × 0.9 = 0.077.
  3. Forum post: 0.085 × 0.4 = 0.034.
  4. Sum: −0.171 + 0.077 + 0.034 = −0.060.

For the second number the same arithmetic gives −0.068 + 0.009 − 0.017 = −0.077. So the loss's rate on q(x) is [−0.060, −0.077], and the formula writes the same arithmetic like this:

∂𝓛 / ∂q(x) = ∑z ( pη(z|x) − p(z|x, y) ) d(z)

Now move q(x) against this rate with a step size of 1: [1.0 + 0.060, 2.0 + 0.077] = [1.060, 2.077]. The new scores are 1.890, 1.161 and 0.009, the weights 0.612, 0.295 and 0.093, the total 0.707, and the loss falls from 0.355 to 0.346.

The office hours page's score also rose, from 1.1 to 1.161, even though its rate said it should fall. All the scores come from the same q(x), so training cannot move them independently. The loss still fell, because the queue log's score rose the most: by 0.090, against 0.061 for the office hours page.

Why the passage vectors stay fixed

The loss also has a rate on d(z). On the queue log's vector it is (prior − posterior) × q(x) = −0.171 × [1.0, 2.0] = [−0.171, −0.342]. In lesson 12, DPR's own training changes the passage encoder with exactly this kind of rate, because no index exists at that point.

RAG does not take this step. If the passage encoder BERTd changed, the vectors of 21 million passages and the index would have to be rebuilt every so often, as REALM does during its pretraining. According to the paper, results were strong without this, so the passage encoder and the index stay fixed. from the paper

From the toy to the real models

In the toy, q(x) itself changed. In reality q(x) = BERTq(x), and carries the same chain rule through every layer of BERTq. The generator's rate reaches every parameter of BART. According to the paper, each BERT encoder has 110 million parameters and BART 406 million, 626 million in total. from the paper The passage encoder's 110 million stay fixed, so training changes about 516 million parameters. my arithmetic

The paper's experiments: freezing the retriever, and swapping in BM25

The paper also measured how much the retriever's learning helps. For this it trained modified models. Such an experiment is called an ablation: remove or freeze one part of the model, and see how far the result drops. In the frozen model the query encoder does not change either. In the BM25 model, lesson 11's BM25 takes DPR's place, and its scores go into the softmax. from the paper

RetrieverRAG-TokenRAG-Sequence

Exact Match on NQ, on the dev set. That is why these differ a little from the test numbers 44.1 and 44.5 in step 5.

  • Freezing the query encoder drops RAG-Sequence from 44.0 to 41.2, a loss of 2.8 points. For RAG-Token the drop is 5.7 points.
  • With BM25, NQ drops by 12 to 14 points: from 44.0 to 31.8, and from 43.5 to 29.7.
  • On FEVER, where a claim has to be judged true or false from Wikipedia, BM25 did best. The paper's guess is that those claims are full of names, and matching words is what BM25 does.

There is also a risk. In the paper's early experiments, on some tasks such as story writing, the retriever started bringing the same passages for every question. The generator then learned to ignore the passages, and RAG behaved like BART on its own. This is called retrieval collapse. The paper's guess is that with long answers the rates reaching the retriever become weak. from the paper

8RAG-Sequence and RAG-Token in depth: posterior, decoding and K

The difference between the two shows up in the posterior: RAG-Token can keep a different posterior at each token, while RAG-Sequence keeps one for the whole answer. The paper writes the two formulas like this, and in our Thursday-Zoom example N = 2, y1 = Thursday and y2 = Zoom: from the paper

pRAG-Sequence(y|x) ≈ ∑z ∈ top-k(p(·|x)) pη(z|x) ∏i=1N pθ(yi | x, z, y1:i−1)
pRAG-Token(y|x) ≈ ∏i=1N ∑z ∈ top-k(p(·|x)) pη(z|x) pθ(yi | x, z, y1:i−1)

y1:i−1 means tokens 1 through i − 1, the y<i of step 3. For Zoom it is just Thursday. top-k(p(·|x)) is the k passages the retriever brought, here the queue log and the office hours page.

A posterior at every token

In RAG-Token each token has its own weighted sum, so each token also has its own posterior. At Thursday, the queue log's is 0.54 ÷ 0.74 = 0.730 and the office hours page's is 0.20 ÷ 0.74 = 0.270. At Zoom they are 0.12 ÷ 0.48 = 0.25 and 0.36 ÷ 0.48 = 0.75. At the first token the queue log leads, and at the second the office hours page does. RAG-Sequence has a single posterior for the whole answer: 0.108 ÷ 0.288 = 0.375 and 0.180 ÷ 0.288 = 0.625.

The paper's Figure 2 shows the same thing on a real example. In Jeopardy the input was Hemingway, and RAG-Token wrote: "The Sun Also Rises" is a novel by this author of "A Farewell to Arms". While writing Sun, the posterior was high for the passage that mentions The Sun Also Rises, and at A Farewell to Arms it was high for the other passage. After the first token of each title the posterior flattens, because BART completes the rest of the title from its own parameters. from the paper

If the answer is a single token, the two formulas become the same, because the ∏ has just one term left. FEVER's labels, such as supports, are single tokens like this, so there RAG-Sequence and RAG-Token are equal. from the paper

Writing the answer: Thorough and Fast decoding

In training the right answer is known in advance, so computing its probability is enough. At test time the model has to write the answer itself, and this is called decoding. RAG-Token takes the summed probability at each token, such as 0.74 at Thursday, and writes token by token like an ordinary generator. For QA the paper picks the token with the highest probability at each step, the of lesson 1. from the paper

RAG-Sequence's probability does not split into tokens, because its sum comes after the whole answer. So the paper gets its answer in three steps: from the paper

  1. With each passage, the generator writes one answer on its own. These answers are the candidates, and K passages give at most K candidates.
  2. For each candidate, the generator runs again with the passages that did not produce it. This gives the candidate's probability with every passage.
  3. Each candidate gets its weighted sum, and the candidate with the largest sum becomes the answer.

This method is called Thorough decoding. Skip step 2, treating a candidate's probability as 0 with every passage that did not produce it, and you get Fast decoding. On NQ, RAG-Sequence takes 50 passages and runs Thorough decoding, because the answers are short. That means up to 50 candidates, each checked with 50 passages, so up to about 50 × 50 = 2,500 generator runs. For tasks with long answers the paper used Fast decoding, because Thorough did not improve the results. the count of 2,500 is mine

How many passages: the effect of K

In training K was 5 or 10, with no significant difference between the two. At test time the paper picks K itself: for NQ, RAG-Token takes 15 passages and RAG-Sequence 50. The paper's Figure 3 shows that with more passages, RAG-Sequence's NQ score rises and then stays about level. RAG-Token's score peaks at around 10 passages and then falls slightly. from the paper

The second graph in that figure shows answer recall, the percentage of questions with an answer passage inside the top K. At every K the line for RAG's learned retriever sits above fixed DPR, and the BM25 line is far below both. So after end-to-end training the retriever brings the right passages higher, which is the result of step 6.

  2. The red note says to weight each passage's answer by how much the retriever trusts that passage. What does that trust look like after seeing the answer, and what does training do with it?

    Show the answer

    The formula's trust is the prior p(z|x). The trust after seeing the answer is the posterior, 0.773 instead of 0.602 for the queue log. Training pulls the prior toward the posterior, because the loss's rate on a score is prior − posterior. (Step 6)

  3. The second slide says Jointly train the retriever and generator. Which part of the retriever is really trained?

3 points from this lesson

  1. RAG treats the passage as a hidden choice and takes a weighted sum over the top-K passages: p(y|x) ≈ ∑ pη(z|x) · pθ(y|x, z). So RAG's own training needs no labels for the right passage.
  2. The loss's rate on each score is prior − posterior, and on each of the generator's ln az it is −posterior. So training pulls the retriever's weights toward the posterior, and the generator learns most with the passage whose posterior is large.
  3. RAG-Token can keep a different posterior at each token, while RAG-Sequence keeps one for the whole answer. The query encoder and the generator are trained, the passage encoder and the index are not. Even so, the learned retriever beats a frozen one by 2.8 points on the NQ dev set, and BM25 by 12.2.

New terms in this lesson

RAG
Retrieval-Augmented Generation: joining a retriever and a generator into one model and training them together with one loss. For example, bringing the queue log and writing Thursday 9am from it.
Latent variable
A hidden choice that the data does not record. In RAG, the choice of which passage the answer came from. For example, a question-answer pair does not say that the answer came from the queue log.
η and θ
The names of the retriever's (eta) and the generator's (theta) parameters. That is why the retriever is written pη(z|x) and the generator pθ(y|x, z).
BERT
An encoder model Google released in 2018. Both of the RAG retriever's encoders, the query encoder and the passage encoder, are built from it.
MIPS
Maximum inner product search: finding the K passages in the index with the largest dot products. For example, the 5 highest scores among 21 million passages.
Generator
The model that reads the question and one passage and writes the answer, pθ(y|x, z). In RAG it is BART. For example, 0.9 for Thursday 9am with the queue log.
Encoder-decoder
The encoder turns the input into vectors, and the decoder looks at them while it writes one token at a time. BART is such a model.
Concatenate
Join two texts end to end into one text. For example, question: … followed by passage: ….
RAG-Sequence
The whole answer with each passage, then the weighted sum. For example, 0.6 × 0.18 + 0.4 × 0.45 = 0.288.
RAG-Token
A weighted sum at every token, then the product of the tokens. For example, 0.74 × 0.48 = 0.355.
End-to-end training
Training all the parts together with one loss. In RAG the query encoder and the generator both learn from the same −ln p(y|x). For example, −ln 0.70 = 0.357.
Backpropagation
Working backwards from the loss to get every parameter's gradient, backprop for short. In RAG it reaches the generator and the query encoder, but not the passage encoder.
Exact Match
An answer counts as right only if it matches the correct answer exactly. The score is the percentage of such questions. For example, RAG-Sequence's 44.5 on NQ.
Closed book and open book
Answering from parameters alone, or searching documents first and then answering. For example, on NQ the closed-book T5-11B scores 34.5 and the open-book RAG-Sequence 44.5.
Posterior and prior
The prior pη(z|x) is a passage's weight before seeing the answer, and the posterior p(z|x, y) is its weight after, that passage's share of the total probability. For example, the queue log's prior is 0.602 and its posterior 0.773.
Ablation
Removing or freezing one part of a model and measuring again, to see what that part contributes. For example, freezing the query encoder in RAG-Sequence drops NQ (dev) from 44.0 to 41.2.
Thorough and Fast decoding
Two ways for RAG-Sequence to write an answer. Each passage produces one candidate. Thorough checks every candidate with every passage, Fast only with the passage that produced it. For example, with 50 passages on NQ, Thorough needs up to about 2,500 generator runs.
Retrieval collapse
The retriever starts bringing the same passages for every question during training, after which the generator learns to ignore them. For example, on the paper's story-writing task.
