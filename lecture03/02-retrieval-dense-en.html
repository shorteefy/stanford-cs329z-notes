Retrieval: finding before answering

Retrieval is the step that finds the useful pieces of your documents when a question arrives, so that an LLM can read them and answer. Two parts share the work: the retriever finds the pieces, and the reader reads them and writes the answer.

The need for it showed up in lesson 1. Putting one line of the queue log into the raised the probability of Thursday from 0.21 to 0.81, but we picked that line ourselves. Now picture a company with 2,000 documents of about 30 paragraphs each, so 2,000 × 30 = 60,000 paragraphs. The from lesson 1 had room for 7,000 tokens of passages. At 500 tokens a paragraph, only 7,000 ÷ 500 = 14 paragraphs fit.

So for every question, some program has to pick the 14 out of 60,000 that hold the answer. This lesson builds such a program in the simplest way: by counting words. Of the course's 3 questions, it handles the first well. On the second it ties a right passage with a wrong one, and on the third it returns nothing.

1Corpus, document and passage: where the retriever looks

The set of all your documents is called the corpus, and the retriever searches inside it. Each single item in the corpus is a document: a web page, a file or an email. Another name for the corpus is the collection.

The retriever does not return a whole document, because the prompt has room for only 14 pieces, and most of a long document has nothing to do with the question. The milestone date in a 40-page syllabus probably takes 2 lines. So the retriever returns a small piece, such as one paragraph, and this piece is called a passage.

The question you are searching for is the query. This lesson's first query is the one from lesson 1: which office hour is least crowded.

Later lessons keep coming back to this small corpus. It has 6 documents, with one passage from each. Every passage also has a short ID, such as P3, so that an answer can name it. the syllabus, queue log, Homework 1 announcement and project page come from the slides, the office hours page and the old forum post are mine

2Retrieval: putting the right passage into the prompt when a question arrives

Retrieval means finding the most useful passages in the corpus when a query arrives, and putting them into the prompt with the question. The model then does not have to memorize everything in advance. Stanford's slide calls this just-in-time: the right text reaches the model exactly when the question does.

For our query the retriever finds the queue log, and the LLM gets a prompt like this: my example

Answer the question using the passage below. Cite the passage ID.
[P3] Queue log, last 3 weeks: Tuesday 4pm office hour averaged 14 students waiting; Thursday 9am office hour averaged 3.
Question: Which office hour is least crowded?

The answer comes back as Thursday 9am, about 3 students ahead of you [P3]. The [P3] at the end says which passage the claim came from. A source written next to an answer is called a citation. With the citation you can open the queue log and check that it really says this.

In lesson 1 all of the model's knowledge sat in its , which caused two problems. Adding a new fact needed training, and the model could not name its source. With retrieval you add a new fact by adding a passage to the corpus, and the citation shows the source. Stanford's slide calls these two benefits Dynamic and Interpretable.

Lesson 1 called knowledge stored inside the parameters . Here the knowledge sits in the corpus, outside the model, and knowledge kept this way is called non-parametric memory.

Retrieval is only as good as the search, though. The end of lesson 1 showed that with the old forum post in the prompt, the model may write Wednesday again. So the next step builds a firm rule for searching.

3The retriever: score every passage, then take the top K

A retriever gives every passage a score for the query, sorts the passages by score, and returns the top K. A score is a number that says how useful a passage is for the query.

The K highest-scoring passages are called the top-K. K is fixed in advance, for example 2 or 100.

The simplest score is word overlap: how many of the query's words also appear in the passage. But words such as which, is and the appear in almost every text, so counting them tells you nothing.

Very common words like these are called stopwords, and the retriever removes them before counting. Remove which and is from the query which office hour is least crowded. Four words are left: office, hour, least and crowded.

The part that reads the query and the top-K passages and writes the answer is called the reader. Today the reader is usually an LLM that was never trained for this particular task and simply follows the instruction in the prompt. The first line of the prompt above, Answer the question using the passage below, is that instruction.

Doing a task from an instruction alone, without training for it, is called zero-shot. Since the reader is usually such a ready-made LLM, lessons 3 to 15 mostly work on making the retriever better.

4The recall ceiling: a passage that never arrives is never read

Now pick the third query in the widget: when can I get help from a TA. After removing stopwords, help and ta are left. The answer is in the office hours page, but that page says TAs, not ta, and no passage contains help. So every score is 0 and the retriever returns nothing. However good the reader is, the office hours page never reaches its prompt.

How often a retriever finds the passage that is needed is, roughly, the retriever's recall. If the right passage reaches the top K for 60 out of 100 questions, the recall is 60%. 60% means 60 out of 100, that is 60 ÷ 100 = 0.6. Lesson 13 shows how to measure recall precisely.

Now add the reader: when it gets the right passage, it answers 90% of questions correctly, that is 0.9 per question. On the 60 questions that gives 60 × 0.9 = 54 correct answers. On the other 100 − 60 = 40 questions, no correct answer can come from the passages. You can now improve either the reader or the retriever. my example

Work out the third row

The retriever now brings the right passage for 80 questions, and the reader is still right 90% of the time. How many correct answers do you get? More or fewer than with the perfect reader?

Show the answer

80 × 0.9 = 72, which is 72 − 60 = 12 more than the perfect reader. Even a perfect reader cannot go past 60, because for the other 40 questions the right passage never reached it.

The perfect reader stops at 60 because of a rule of multiplication. Multiply a number by something smaller than 1 and it shrinks, as in 60 × 0.5 = 30. Multiply it by 1 and it stays the same, as in 60 × 1.0 = 60. The reader's share can never be above 1, because 1 means every question answered correctly.

So the correct answers can never exceed the 60 questions for which the retriever brought the right passage. The retriever's recall becomes the upper limit of the whole system.

This limit is called the recall ceiling. Stanford's slide writes Missing evidence limits agent under it: evidence that never reaches the prompt holds the system back. Agents come in lesson 16.

A larger K raises recall, but wrong passages come along too, like the old forum post at K = 2. And the company's prompt cannot hold more than 14 passages anyway.

Word overlap failed twice, even on 6 passages. The first time it ranked a right and a wrong passage equally. The second time the query and the passage said the same thing in different words, help from a TA and run by the TAs, and the score was 0. Lesson 3 starts right here: turning text into numbers so that texts saying similar things get numbers close to each other.

5Retriever and reader as functions: the slide's symbols

Stanford's slide writes the retriever and the reader as two functions: the retriever turns the corpus and the query into K passages, and the reader turns the query and those passages into an answer. To read it you first need what a function is.

A function is a rule that takes an input and gives an output. If the rule is “double it and add 1”, you write it as f(x) = 2x + 1. Here f is the name of the rule and x is the input, so f(3) = 2 × 3 + 1 = 7. A function in code does the same thing: it takes arguments and returns a value.

A function can also take more than one input. In f(x, y) = x + 2y, f(1, 3) = 1 + 2 × 3 = 7. Both lines on the slide are functions with two inputs, and “⟶” means “gives as output”:

Retrieverf(𝒟, Q) ⟶ P1, …, PK
Readerg(Q, {P1, …, PK}) ⟶ A

The input has documents (D) and the output has passages (P), because, as step 1 said, a retriever returns pieces of documents. On our query the two functions run in three steps:

  1. f(𝒟, Q): the retriever scores every passage. In corpus order the scores are 0, 1, 2, 2, 0, 0.
  2. With K = 2 the top two passages come back: P1 = queue log and P2 = old forum post. Both score 2, so corpus order decided here.
  3. g(Q, {queue log, old forum post}): the reader reads both passages and writes A. The set holds two passages that contradict each other, so A may say Thursday 9am or Wednesday.

A reading comprehension problem: the reader's job

A reading comprehension problem! is what the slide writes next to the reader line. Reading comprehension means answering a question about a text you are given. After the retriever has run, that is exactly the reader's job, because the passages are already in its hands.

The best-known dataset for this task is SQuAD, the Stanford Question Answering Dataset. Its makers showed people a Wikipedia paragraph, and the people wrote questions about that paragraph. The answer is always a piece of the same paragraph, and such a piece is called a span. The training part has 87,599 questions. from the paper One example:

Paragraph: … At the beginning of the 17th century the empire contained 32 provinces and numerous vassal states. …
Q: How many provinces did the Ottoman empire contain in the 17th century?
A: 32

The rest of the slide: a fixed retriever, and two kinds of reader

The slide has two more lines at the bottom. The first is about the retriever: A standard TF-IDF information-retrieval sparse model (a fixed module). It has three parts:

  • matches words like word overlap does, but it does not count every word equally. A word that appears in few documents gets more weight. Least appears only in the forum post and office appears in two passages, so least weighs more than office. Lesson 11 has the full arithmetic.
  • means a passage is seen as a long list with one number for every word in the vocabulary, and most of those numbers are 0. Our 6 passages have 69 different words, and the old forum post has 14 of them. So its list has 0 in 69 − 14 = 55 places.
  • A fixed module is not trained. Its rule is written in advance and does not change with data. Our word overlap is fixed too: for the same query the scores always come out the same. The DPR retriever in lesson 12 is the opposite, a retriever that is trained.

The second line is about the reader: a neural reading comprehension model. A neural model is a model with parameters that learns from examples, like the LLM in lesson 1. The slide gives two routes for such a reader:

  • Could be trained on SQuAD and other distantly-supervised QA datasets: train the reader on data such as SQuAD. Distantly supervised data comes in step 6.
  • Or a zero-shot LLM (ChatGPT etc): the reader from step 3, which writes the answer from an instruction without being trained for this task.

6DrQA: a retriever and a reader over all of Wikipedia

DrQA is a 2017 system that answers a question by searching all of English Wikipedia. Danqi Chen and colleagues built it at Facebook AI Research. from the paper Stanford's slide shows it as an example of the retriever-reader design. The slide's question is How many of Warsaw's inhabitants spoke Polish in 1933?, and the answer is 833,500.

Here the question can be about any topic, and the system is not handed the right paragraph. Question answering like this is called open-domain QA. In SQuAD-style reading comprehension the paragraph comes with the question. In open-domain QA the system has to find it in the whole corpus itself.

DrQA's retriever

DrQA's corpus is English Wikipedia from 21 December 2016. After some pages such as lists were removed, 5,075,182 articles were left, so N = 5,075,182. The retriever is not trained, and the paper itself calls it non-machine learning. It treats the question and every article as lists of TF-IDF word weights and scores every article from these lists. It returns 5 articles per question, so K = 5. from the paper

Besides single words, this retriever also matches pairs of words that appear side by side. Two adjacent words are called a bigram. The bigrams of our query are which office, office hour, hour is, is least and least crowded.

These articles hold 9,008,962 different words, and far more different bigrams. So DrQA uses a hash function to give each bigram the number of one of 224 buckets. A hash function is a fixed rule that turns any text into a number within a fixed range. 224 means 2 multiplied by itself 24 times, which is 16,777,216 buckets. from the paper

DrQA's reader

DrQA's reader is a neural network trained on SQuAD. It gives every token of a paragraph two probabilities: that the answer starts here, and that the answer ends here. It then picks the piece whose start and end probabilities have the largest product, with no more than 15 tokens between start and end. from the paper

So DrQA's reader does not make up an answer: it picks a span from the text it was given. Step 7 needs this fact for the ceiling. On SQuAD questions that were not used in training, with the right paragraph given, this reader's is 69.5%. Exact Match counts an answer as correct only if it matches the right answer exactly. from the paper

Distant supervision: training data without paragraph labels

Distant supervision means building training examples for data that has only questions and answers, by finding the right paragraph automatically. SQuAD gives every question its paragraph, but CuratedTREC, WebQuestions and WikiMovies give only question-answer pairs. The reader has to learn to pick a span from a paragraph, so it needs paragraphs. This is what the slide means by distantly-supervised QA datasets.

Take one pair on our corpus: the question when is the project milestone due and the answer Week 6, Thursday 11:59pm. No passage carries a label. my example

  1. Run the retriever on the question: the syllabus gets 3, the project page 2, and the other four get 0.
  2. Of the returned passages, drop those that do not contain the answer text exactly. The project page does not contain Week 6, Thursday 11:59pm, so it goes.
  3. The syllabus that is left becomes a training example: the question, the syllabus, and the answer span inside it.

DrQA did this at scale and added a few filters. For each question it took the retriever's top 5 articles and dropped paragraphs without an exact match of the answer. It also dropped paragraphs shorter than 25 or longer than 1,500 characters. If the question contained a name, such as Chadwick, paragraphs without that name were dropped too. It then counted the question's words and bigrams in a 20-token window around the answer and kept at most 5 paragraphs. from the paper

No person labelled the paragraph. The label came automatically from the answer text, which is why this is called distant, meaning indirect, supervision. An answer string can also appear in a paragraph by coincidence, so such a label is sometimes wrong. my caution

7The recall ceiling in real numbers, and the full arithmetic of K

DrQA shows the recall ceiling clearly, because its reader can only pick a span from the text it was given. If the answer text is not in the 5 articles at all, the reader cannot produce it in any way. The retriever brings an article with the answer for 77.8% of SQuAD questions, so the whole system can be right on at most 77.8% of them. numbers from the paper, the ceiling arithmetic is mine

The paper measured the reader in several situations. On the SQuAD questions the results are: from the paper

What the reader gotExact Match (%)
Only the right paragraph, as in SQuAD69.5
The whole right article49.4
The retriever's 5 articles, from all of Wikipedia29.8 with a reader trained on SQuAD and the distantly supervised data together27.1

The ceiling, the percentage of questions for which the retriever brought an article with the answer: 77.8.

Now apply the multiplication from step 4. If the reader were as good with 5 articles as with one right paragraph, the correct answers would be 0.695 of the 77.8%. my arithmetic

  1. Turn both percentages into shares: 77.8% = 0.778 and 69.5% = 0.695.
  2. Multiply: 0.778 × 0.695 = 0.541, which is 54.1%.
  3. Compare with the real result: 54.1 ÷ 27.1 = 2.0, so the real result is half of this estimate.

The paper gives two reasons for the drop. First, the reader has to read 5 whole articles instead of one paragraph, and many sentences on the same topic look like answers. That is why even the right article brings the number down from 69.5 to 49.4. Second, people wrote the SQuAD questions while looking at one specific paragraph, so without that paragraph many questions read as incomplete. from the paper

So the recall ceiling is only an upper limit: the system can land anywhere below it. In DrQA the ceiling is 77.8 and the real result is 27.1. The questions the retriever missed set the ceiling. The reader's weakness makes the gap between the ceiling and the real result.

3 points from this lesson

  1. Retrieval: for every query, pick a few passages from the corpus and put them into the prompt. It needs no training, and the answer comes with a citation.
  2. The retriever f(𝒟, Q) gives the top-K passages, and the reader g(Q, {P1, …, PK}) writes the answer from them. Word overlap gives the queue log and the old forum post the same score, 2, and bigrams do not break the tie either.
  3. A passage the retriever did not bring is never read, and that is the recall ceiling. In DrQA the ceiling is 77.8%, yet the whole system reaches 27.1%, because the ceiling is only an upper limit.

New terms in this lesson

Corpus (collection)
The set of all documents. For example, the company's 2,000 documents, or this course's 6 short texts.
Document
One item in the corpus: a web page, a file or an email. For example, the course syllabus.
Passage
A small piece of a document, which the retriever returns. For example, the syllabus paragraph that holds the milestone date.
Query
The question you search for. For example, “which office hour is least crowded”.
Retrieval
Finding useful passages for a query and putting them into the prompt. For example, the queue log going into the prompt with the question.
Citation
The source written next to an answer. For example, the [P3] in “Thursday 9am [P3]”, which points to the queue log.
Non-parametric memory
Knowledge kept outside the model, in the corpus. For example, the queue log: changing it needs no training.
Retriever
The part that scores every passage and returns the top K. For example, this lesson's word-overlap retriever.
Score
A number for how useful a passage is for the query. For example, the queue log's word-overlap score of 2.
Top-K
The K passages with the highest scores. For example, the queue log and the old forum post at K = 2.
Word overlap
How many of the query's words appear in the passage. The simplest score. For example, the queue log has office and hour, so its score is 2.
Stopwords
Very common words such as is, the and which, left out of the count. From “which office hour is least crowded”, which and is are removed.
Reader
The part that reads the query and the top-K passages and writes the answer. Today usually an LLM. For example, reading the queue log and writing “Thursday 9am”.
Zero-shot
Doing a task from an instruction alone, without training on that task. For example, the prompt line “Answer the question using the passage below”.
Recall
How often the retriever finds the passage that is needed. For example, 60 out of 100 questions gives a recall of 60%.
Recall ceiling
The retriever's recall becomes the upper limit of the whole system. With a recall of 60%, no reader can get more than 60 of 100 answers from the passages.
Reading comprehension
Answering a question about a text you are given. This is the reader's job after the retriever. For example, reading the Ottoman empire paragraph and answering 32.
SQuAD
Stanford Question Answering Dataset: questions people wrote about Wikipedia paragraphs, with the answer always a span of the paragraph. For example, 87,599 training questions.
Open-domain QA
A question on any topic that comes without its paragraph, so the system has to find it in the whole corpus. For example, DrQA's Warsaw question over all of Wikipedia.
DrQA
A 2017 retriever-reader system: a fixed TF-IDF retriever returns 5 Wikipedia articles, and a neural reader picks a span from them. For example, 833,500 for the Warsaw question.
Bigram
Two words that appear side by side. For example, office hour in the queue log, and least crowded in the forum post.
Distant supervision
Finding paragraphs automatically for data that has only questions and answers: run the retriever and keep the passages that contain the answer text exactly. For example, the syllabus for the milestone question.
